High-level synthesis of the [[MGMT 309]] knowledge base. Currently covers Chapter 1 of [[Ricky W. Griffin]], *Fundamentals of Management*, 11th edition.

## Core Thesis

Management is defined as achieving organizational goals **efficiently and effectively** through a set of activities directed at an organization's resources. Everything else in the foundational chapter elaborates one of four questions:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What do managers do? | Four functions: plan, organize, lead, control |
| What kinds of managers exist? | Three levels × six functional areas |
| What must managers be good at? | Seven skills |
| How do we know any of this? | Five historical and contemporary perspectives |

## The Chapter 1 Map

**Definitions.** [[Organization]] and [[Management]] anchor the course. The efficient/effective pair is the first and most-tested distinction: doing things right versus doing the right things, with both required.

**Classification.** [[Kinds of Managers]] sorts managers on two independent dimensions — level (top, middle, first-line) and area (marketing, financial, operations, HR, administrative, other). Level and area cross-cut; a job title places a manager on both at once.

**Process.** [[Basic Management Functions]] gives the four-function cycle. Planning sets goals, organizing arranges resources, leading mobilizes people, controlling measures progress and feeds back into planning.

**Capability.** [[Fundamental Management Skills]] lists seven skills — technical, interpersonal, conceptual, diagnostic, communication, decision-making, time management. Functions are what managers *do*; skills are what they must *be good at*. Do not mix the lists.

**Epistemology.** [[The Science and Art of Management]] splits those seven skills across a rational-analytical half and an intuitive-experiential half, and insists managers need both.

## The Historical Argument

The single most useful thing to understand about Chapter 1 is that the five perspectives form a **chain of corrections**, not a list of options. See [[The Evolution of Management Thought]].

The [[Classical Management Perspective]] is given three specific limitations, and each one generates a successor:

| Classical limitation | Successor school |
|---|---|
| Slighted the role of the individual | [[Behavioral Management Perspective]] |
| Proposed universal guidelines that do not fit every organization | [[Contingency Perspective]] |
| Focused on stable, simple organizations | [[Systems Perspective]] — organizations are *open* |

The [[Quantitative Management Perspective]] is the fourth branch: it keeps the classical ambition of systematic method but upgrades the tooling from time-and-motion study to mathematical models and computers.

## The Empirical Hinge

The [[Hawthorne Studies]] are where the history turns. Under a piecework incentive plan, a work group **informally capped its own output** and enforced that cap socially — labeling overproducers *rate busters* and underproducers *chiselers*.

Punishing overproduction is the detail that matters. If workers maximized earnings, exceeding the norm would be admired. That it was sanctioned falsifies the economic-incentive model behind [[Frederick W. Taylor|Taylor's]] piecework system, and opens the way for [[Theory X and Theory Y]] and the human relations movement.

## Standing Tensions

The chapter leaves two oppositions deliberately unresolved, and both recur later in the course:

- **Behavioral vs. quantitative** — reappearing as the art and science of management. The resolution offered is blending, not choosing.
- **Universal vs. contingency** — whether management knowledge yields general prescriptions or situation-dependent ones. The contingency view wins on the page, but the course continues to teach general frameworks, so the tension is practical rather than settled.

## Contemporary Conditions

[[Contemporary Management Challenges]] closes the chapter with globalization, ethical scandals, quality, the service economy, recession aftermath, workforce diversity, organizational change, and social media. Every item is an **environmental** factor — which is simultaneously the argument for the systems view (organizations are open) and for the contingency view (situations vary, so prescriptions must too).
