## Key Definitions Introduced

- **Organization** — a group of people working together in a structured and coordinated fashion to achieve a set of goals
- **Management** — a set of activities directed at an organization's resources, aimed at achieving organizational goals efficiently and effectively
- **Manager** — someone whose primary responsibility is to carry out the management process
- **Efficient** — using resources wisely and cost-effectively
- **Effective** — making the right decisions and successfully implementing them
- **Soldiering** — employees deliberately working at a slow pace
- **System / open system / closed system / subsystem / synergy / entropy** — the [[Systems Perspective]] vocabulary
- **Universal perspective / contingency perspective** — see [[Contingency Perspective]]

## The Chapter's Central Argument

Beyond the definitions, the chapter makes one connected argument worth stating plainly:

The [[Classical Management Perspective]] is presented with three specific limitations — it focused on stable, simple organizations; it proposed universal guidelines that do not fit every organization; and it slighted the role of the individual. **Each limitation generates a successor school.** The neglected individual produces the [[Behavioral Management Perspective]]; the false universalism produces the [[Contingency Perspective]]; the assumption of a stable, self-contained organization produces the [[Systems Perspective]] and its insistence that organizations are open.

The [[Hawthorne Studies]] are the empirical hinge: a work group under a piecework plan capped its own output and punished overproducers, which no economic-incentive model could explain.
