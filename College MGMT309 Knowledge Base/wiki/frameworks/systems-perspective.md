The **systems perspective** views the organization as a **system** — an interrelated set of elements functioning as a whole. It is one of the two contemporary management perspectives, alongside the [[Contingency Perspective]], and it supplies a compact vocabulary that recurs throughout the course.

## The Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **System** | An interrelated set of elements functioning as a whole |
| **Open system** | A system that interacts with its environment |
| **Closed system** | A system that does not interact with its environment |
| **Subsystem** | A system within a broader system |
| **Synergy** | Two or more subsystems working together to produce more than the total of what they might produce working alone |
| **Entropy** | A normal process leading to system decline |

These six terms are the most definition-dense passage in the chapter and are prime exam material.

## Organizations as Open Systems

The critical classification: organizations are **open** systems. They take inputs from their environment and return outputs to it, which means environmental change is not an external disturbance to be ignored but a permanent condition of operating.

This is why top managers in [[Kinds of Managers]] are described as officially representing the organization to the external environment — managing the boundary between system and environment is a distinct executive responsibility.

A closed system, by contrast, would be self-contained. Treating an organization as closed is precisely the error the [[Classical Management Perspective]] made in assuming stable, simple organizations.

## Synergy

Synergy is the systems-theoretic justification for organizing at all. If subsystems working together produced no more than the sum of their separate outputs, there would be no reason to coordinate them — and coordination is the defining feature of an [[Organization]]. Synergy is what the organizing function in [[Basic Management Functions]] is trying to capture.

## Entropy

Entropy is described as a **normal process** leading to system decline — not an accident or a failure, but the default trajectory. The managerial implication follows directly: an organization left alone runs down. Resisting entropy requires continuous input from the environment, which only an open system can obtain.

This gives the [[Basic Management Functions|controlling]] function a deeper rationale than mere goal-tracking: monitoring exists partly because decline is the baseline state.
