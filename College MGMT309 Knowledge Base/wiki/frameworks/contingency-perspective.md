The **contingency perspective** suggests that appropriate managerial behavior in a given situation depends on — is *contingent on* — unique elements in that situation. It is defined against the **universal perspective**, which attempts to identify the one best way to do something.

This is the second of the two contemporary perspectives, alongside the [[Systems Perspective]], and arguably the most consequential idea in the chapter.

## The Core Contrast

| Perspective | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Universal** | There is one best way to do something; find it and apply it everywhere |
| **Contingency** | The appropriate action depends on the specific situation |

The contingency view does not say that management knowledge is useless or that anything goes. It says that prescriptions carry **conditions** — a technique that works in one setting may fail in another, and the manager's job includes diagnosing which setting they are in.

## Why the Classical School Was Universalist

The [[Classical Management Perspective]]'s acknowledged failure is stated in exactly these terms: it *proposed universal guidelines that we now recognize do not fit every organization.* [[Frederick W. Taylor|Taylor's]] one best way to perform a task and [[Henri Fayol|Fayol's]] general principles of management were both universalist in ambition.

The contingency perspective is the direct correction to that error, which is why the chapter places it last.

## Consequences for the Rest of the Course

The contingency view reframes several earlier ideas:

- **Skills**: the [[Fundamental Management Skills|diagnostic skill]] — visualizing the most appropriate response to a situation — is essentially the contingency perspective expressed as a personal capability.
- **Science and art**: if one best way existed, the science half of [[The Science and Art of Management]] would suffice. The permanent need for judgment is a consequence of contingency.
- **Later chapters**: motivation, leadership, and structure are each presented as families of options selected to fit circumstances, rather than single correct answers.

## A Caution

The contingency perspective is easy to state and easy to abuse — "it depends" is not an analysis. Its useful form specifies *what* it depends on: organization size, environmental stability, technology, workforce characteristics, and so forth. Exam answers invoking contingency should name the contingent factors rather than stopping at the slogan.
