The **Hawthorne studies** are the empirical foundation of the [[Behavioral Management Perspective]]. Their significance is that they produced results the [[Classical Management Perspective]] could not explain, and in doing so shifted management thought toward the social context of work.

## The Illumination Study

The first study manipulated **illumination** for one group of workers and compared their subsequent productivity against a control group whose lighting was unchanged.

The design is a straightforward controlled experiment on a physical working condition — exactly the sort of variable scientific management would expect to drive output.

## The Piecework Incentive Study

Another study introduced a **piecework incentive plan** for nine men assembling terminal banks.

- **Scientific management predicted maximum output.** Under piecework, each worker earns more by producing more, so the rational prediction is that everyone maximizes.
- **What actually happened**: the group *informally set an acceptable level of output* and enforced it socially.

The group's enforcement vocabulary is specific and exam-relevant:

| Term | Who it labels |
|---|---|
| **Rate busters** | Workers who produced *above* the group's accepted level |
| **Chiselers** | Workers who produced *below* the group's accepted level |

Both are pejorative. The group punished deviation in **either** direction — which is the crucial detail. If workers were maximizing individual earnings, overproduction would be admired, not sanctioned. The existence of a penalty for overproducing is what makes the result impossible to reconcile with a purely economic model of worker behavior.

## Why This Matters

The studies undermined the assumption behind [[Frederick W. Taylor|Taylor's]] piecework system: that pay tied to output reliably elicits maximum output. Workers turned out to respond to **group norms** — the social context of the workplace — even at direct cost to their own earnings.

This finding is what the **human relations movement** was built on, and it is the reason the behavioral perspective treats attitudes, group processes, and interpersonal dynamics as first-class managerial concerns rather than noise.
