Management thought developed through three historical perspectives — **classical**, **behavioral**, and **quantitative** — followed by two contemporary ones, **systems** and **contingency**. This page is the map; each perspective has its own page.

The chapter's organizing device is that **each perspective is paired with a critique**, and each critique explains why the next perspective was needed. The schools are not a list of independent options but a chain of corrections.

## The Five Perspectives

| Perspective | Central concern | Key figures |
|---|---|---|
| [[Classical Management Perspective]] | Individual worker performance (scientific) and total organization (administrative) | [[Frederick W. Taylor]], [[Henri Fayol]], [[Max Weber]] |
| [[Behavioral Management Perspective]] | Individual attitudes, behaviors, and group processes | [[Hugo Münsterberg]], [[Mary Parker Follett]], [[Douglas McGregor]] |
| [[Quantitative Management Perspective]] | Decision making, cost-effectiveness, mathematical models, computers | — |
| [[Systems Perspective]] | The organization as an interrelated whole interacting with its environment | — |
| [[Contingency Perspective]] | Appropriate behavior depends on the situation | — |

## The Chain of Corrections

**Classical → Behavioral.** The classical school *slighted the role of the individual in organizations*. The [[Hawthorne Studies]] then produced results it could not explain — a work group socially enforcing an output ceiling against its own economic interest. The behavioral perspective formed around what the classical school omitted: people.

**Classical → Quantitative.** The classical school pursued systematic, rational method but had only time-and-motion study to work with. The quantitative perspective kept the ambition and upgraded the tooling to mathematical models and computers.

**Classical → Contingency.** The classical school *proposed universal guidelines that we now recognize do not fit every organization.* The [[Contingency Perspective]] is the explicit rejection of that universalism.

**Classical → Systems.** The classical school *focused on stable, simple organizations* — effectively treating them as closed systems. The [[Systems Perspective]] insists organizations are **open**, permanently exposed to environmental change.

Notice that all three classical limitations, plus the tooling gap, each generate a successor school. The critique of the classical perspective is the engine of the entire history.

## Behavioral vs. Quantitative: The Standing Tension

These two are not reconciled by the chapter; they are held in tension and reappear as [[The Science and Art of Management]]:

- Quantitative → the **science** of management: rational, logical, objective, systematic.
- Behavioral → the **art** of management: intuition, experience, instinct, personal insight.

The resolution offered is not that one wins but that managers must blend both.

## Do the Old Perspectives Still Apply?

Yes — the chapter's "…Today" sections assess each as still partially valid rather than discarded. Bureaucracy still describes real organizations, piecework incentives still exist, group norms still govern output, and quantitative models are expanding into new domains. What changed is the claim to universality, not the content.
