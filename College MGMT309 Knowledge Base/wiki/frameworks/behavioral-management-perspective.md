The **behavioral management perspective** emphasizes individual attitudes, behaviors, and group processes. It arose to fill the gap the [[Classical Management Perspective]] left open — the classical school's acknowledged failure to account for the role of the individual in organizations.

## Early Contributors

- **[[Hugo Münsterberg]]** (1863–1916), the *father of industrial psychology*, suggested that psychologists could make valuable contributions to managers in employee selection, training, and motivation.
- **[[Mary Parker Follett]]** (1868–1933) anticipated the behavioral management perspective, working ahead of the school that would later formalize her concerns.

## The Hawthorne Studies

The empirical turning point. See [[Hawthorne Studies]] for detail; in brief, the studies produced results that scientific management could not explain — most pointedly, a work group that **informally set its own acceptable level of output** rather than maximizing earnings under a piecework incentive, enforcing that norm socially against *rate busters* who overproduced and *chiselers* who underproduced.

That finding is the hinge of the whole chapter: it is direct evidence against the economic-incentive assumption underpinning [[Frederick W. Taylor|Taylor's]] piecework system.

## The Human Relations Movement

Proposed that workers respond primarily to the **social context of the workplace** — social conditioning, group norms, and interpersonal dynamics — rather than to economic incentive alone.

The movement's central contribution to the course vocabulary is the pair of contrasting assumption sets about human nature, [[Theory X and Theory Y]]:

- **Theory X**: a pessimistic and negative view of workers, consistent with the views of scientific management.
- **Theory Y**: a positive view of workers, representing the assumptions that human relations advocates make.

Note the alignment the chapter draws explicitly: Theory X is not a neutral alternative but the implicit anthropology of the classical school.

## Contemporary Behavioral Science: Organizational Behavior

**Organizational behavior (OB)** is the contemporary field focusing on behavioral perspectives on management. Its subject matter includes job satisfaction, stress, motivation, leadership, group dynamics, organizational politics, interpersonal conflict, and the structure and design of organizations.

OB is the modern descendant of the human relations movement, distinguished from it by being a research discipline rather than a reform program.

## The Behavioral Perspective Today

Managers are now more likely to recognize the importance of behavioral processes and to **view employees as valuable resources rather than mere tools**.

That phrasing marks the shift the perspective accomplished: the classical school treated labor as an input to be optimized; the behavioral school treats people as the organization's substance.
