**Theory X** and **Theory Y** are two contrasting sets of assumptions about human nature at work, formulated by [[Douglas McGregor]] in *The Human Side of Enterprise* (1960). They emerged from the human relations movement within the [[Behavioral Management Perspective]].

The pair is not a theory of what workers *are* — it is a theory about what **managers assume** workers are, and how those assumptions shape managerial behavior.

## Theory X — The Pessimistic View

A pessimistic and negative view of workers, consistent with the views of scientific management.

Its assumptions, in substance:

- People dislike work and try to avoid it.
- Because people dislike work, managers must control, direct, coerce, and threaten employees to get them to work toward organizational goals.
- People prefer to be directed, wish to avoid responsibility, want security, and have little ambition.

## Theory Y — The Positive View

A positive view of workers, representing the assumptions that human relations advocates make.

Its assumptions, in substance:

- People do not naturally dislike work; work is a natural part of their lives.
- People are internally motivated to reach objectives to which they are committed.
- Commitment to goals is proportional to the personal rewards received for reaching them.
- People will both seek and accept responsibility under favorable conditions.
- People have the capacity to be innovative in solving organizational problems.
- People are bright, but under most organizational conditions their potential is underutilized.

## The Structural Contrast

| Dimension | Theory X | Theory Y |
|---|---|---|
| Attitude toward work | Dislike, avoidance | Natural part of life |
| Source of motivation | External — coercion, control, threat | Internal — commitment to objectives |
| Responsibility | Avoided | Sought and accepted |
| Ambition / capability | Little ambition | Bright, innovative, underutilized |
| Manager's required role | Direct and control | Create favorable conditions |

Note the asymmetry: Theory Y has more assumptions than Theory X, and its final assumption is a claim about **organizations** rather than about people — that most organizational conditions waste human potential. That is a critique of management, not of workers.

## Why It Aligns With the Historical Schools

The chapter states directly that Theory X is *consistent with the views of scientific management*. This is the clearest link between the assumption sets and the historical perspectives:

- [[Classical Management Perspective]] → operates on Theory X assumptions, hence piecework incentives, close supervision, and the treatment of labor as an input to be optimized.
- [[Behavioral Management Perspective]] → operates on Theory Y assumptions, hence attention to attitudes, group processes, and employees as valuable resources.

## The Self-Fulfilling Quality

The framework's practical bite is that assumptions drive behavior which drives outcomes. A manager holding Theory X assumptions builds coercive control systems, which suppress initiative and responsibility-seeking — producing the very behavior that appears to confirm Theory X. The [[Hawthorne Studies]] supplied the empirical opening for taking Theory Y seriously.
