The chapter identifies **seven fundamental management skills**: technical, interpersonal, conceptual, diagnostic, communication, decision-making, and time management. These are capabilities a manager must possess, as distinct from the activities a manager performs — see [[Basic Management Functions]] for that separate list.

## The Seven Skills

| Skill | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Technical** | The skills necessary to accomplish or understand the specific kind of work done in an organization |
| **Interpersonal** | The ability to communicate with, understand, and motivate both individuals and groups |
| **Conceptual** | The manager's ability to think in the abstract |
| **Diagnostic** | The manager's ability to visualize the most appropriate response to a situation |
| **Communication** | The manager's ability both to convey ideas and information to others effectively and to receive ideas and information from others effectively |
| **Decision-making** | The ability to correctly recognize and define problems and opportunities, then select an appropriate course of action to solve problems and capitalize on opportunities |
| **Time management** | The ability to prioritize work, work efficiently, and delegate appropriately |

## Notes on Individual Skills

**Technical skills** are singled out as most important for *first-line managers*, who spend much of their time training subordinates and must understand the work being done. This is the clearest link between the skills list and the levels in [[Kinds of Managers]].

**Interpersonal skills** are framed in terms of direction: a manager must get along with subordinates, peers, *and* those at higher levels. The skill is not only downward-facing.

**Conceptual skills** are explicitly about the "big picture" — the mental capacity to understand the overall workings of the organization and its environment, think strategically, and make broad-based decisions.

**Communication skills** are explicitly two-directional: transmitting ideas so subordinates know what is expected, *and* listening to understand the real meaning behind what others say. Half of the skill is receiving.

**Decision-making skills** carry a realistic standard — effective managers make good decisions *most of the time*, not always.

## Conceptual vs. Diagnostic

The pair most easily confused. Conceptual is about **abstract understanding** — seeing how the whole organization and its environment fit together. Diagnostic is about **situational response** — seeing what to do about a specific circumstance. Conceptual reads the system; diagnostic prescribes the action.

## Skills and the Science/Art Split

The seven skills divide cleanly across the two halves of [[The Science and Art of Management]]:

- **Science side**: technical, diagnostic, decision-making
- **Art side**: conceptual, communication, interpersonal, time management

This mapping is stated directly in the chapter and is a natural exam question.
