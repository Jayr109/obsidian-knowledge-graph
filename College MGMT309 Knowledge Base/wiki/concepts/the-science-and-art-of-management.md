Management is presented as **both a science and an art** — not one or the other. The science side approaches problems rationally, logically, objectively, and systematically; the art side draws on intuition, experience, instinct, and personal insight. The chapter's conclusion is that effective managers must blend the two.

## The Science of Management

Management problems and issues can be approached in ways that are rational, logical, objective, and systematic. Managers use quantitative models and decision-making techniques to arrive at "correct" decisions.

The scare quotes around *correct* are the chapter's own and are worth preserving — they signal that even the scientific side does not promise certainty.

**Skills used**: technical, diagnostic, decision-making.

**Intellectual home**: the [[Quantitative Management Perspective]], which is built on mathematical models, cost-effectiveness analysis, and computers.

## The Art of Management

Managers make decisions and solve problems on the basis of intuition, experience, instinct, and personal insight.

**Skills used**: conceptual, communication, interpersonal, time management.

**Intellectual home**: the [[Behavioral Management Perspective]], which deals in attitudes, group processes, and human relations — matters that resist reduction to a model.

## The Synthesis

The chapter does not present these as competing camps to choose between. Managers must **blend an element of intuition and personal insight with hard data and objective facts.** Neither alone is sufficient: pure analysis ignores the human and situational texture of real decisions, while pure instinct ignores information that is genuinely available.

## Mapping to the Skills

| Science side | Art side |
|---|---|
| Technical | Conceptual |
| Diagnostic | Communication |
| Decision-making | Interpersonal |
| | Time management |

Four of the seven skills in [[Fundamental Management Skills]] fall on the art side and three on the science side — a distribution worth noticing, given that introductory treatments of management often read as though the discipline were purely analytical.

## Connection to the Contingency Perspective

The science/art duality anticipates the [[Contingency Perspective]]: if there were one best way to manage, the science side alone would suffice and management could be reduced to executing the correct model. The persistent need for judgment is a symptom of the fact that appropriate managerial behavior depends on the situation.
