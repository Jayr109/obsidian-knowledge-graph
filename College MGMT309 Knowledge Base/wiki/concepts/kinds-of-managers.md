Managers are classified along two independent dimensions: **by level** in the hierarchy (top, middle, first-line) and **by area** of the organization (marketing, financial, operations, human resource, administrative). Every manager sits at some intersection of the two — a plant manager is a middle manager in operations.

## By Level

### Top Managers

The relatively small group of executives who manage the overall organization.

- **Titles**: president, vice president, CEO
- **Responsibilities**: create the organization's goals, overall strategy, and operating policies; officially represent the organization to the external environment — meeting with government officials, executives of other organizations, and similar outside parties

Note the outward-facing half of the role: top managers are the organization's interface to its environment, which is what the [[Systems Perspective]] would call managing the boundary of an open system.

### Middle Managers

The **largest group** of managers in most organizations.

- **Titles**: plant manager, operations manager, division head
- **Responsibilities**: implement the policies and plans developed by top managers; supervise and coordinate the activities of lower-level managers

Middle managers are the translation layer — they convert strategy from above into operations below, and manage other managers rather than operating employees.

### First-Line Managers

Supervise and coordinate the activities of **operating employees**.

- **Titles**: supervisor, coordinator, office manager
- **Responsibilities**: oversee day-to-day operations; hire operating employees; handle routine administrative duties; spend a large proportion of their time directly supervising subordinates' work

The distinguishing feature is *who they supervise*: first-line managers supervise non-managers, which is why their time goes to direct supervision and why [[Fundamental Management Skills|technical skills]] matter most at this level.

## By Area

| Area | Concerned with |
|---|---|
| **Marketing managers** | Areas related to the marketing function — new product development, promotion, and distribution |
| **Financial managers** | The organization's financial resources — accounting, cash management, investments |
| **Operations managers** | Creating and managing the systems that produce the organization's products and services — production control, inventory control, quality control, plant layout, site selection |
| **Human resource managers** | Hiring and developing employees — HR planning, recruiting and selecting, training and development, designing compensation and benefit systems |
| **Administrative (general) managers** | Not associated with any particular specialty; generalists with basic familiarity across all functional areas rather than specialized training in one |
| **Other managers** | Public relations managers, research and development (R&D) managers, internal consultants |

**Administrative managers are defined by what they are not** — the absence of a specialty is the defining feature, not a gap in the taxonomy.

## The Two Dimensions Are Independent

Level and area cross-cut. Knowing someone is a financial manager says nothing about whether they are top, middle, or first-line; knowing someone is a middle manager says nothing about their functional area. Exam questions frequently supply a job title and ask for placement on both dimensions at once.
