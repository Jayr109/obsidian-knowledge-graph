This transcript covers the PE (Price-to-Earnings) model of stock valuation from Section 8.4 of the course textbook, and cautions students strongly against relying on it. The fundamental message is that PE ratios are a rough ballpark tool only, not a rigorous valuation method.

## The PE Model

**Method:** Find comparable companies in the same industry, calculate their PE ratios (stock price / earnings per share), average those ratios, and multiply by the target company's EPS.

**Formula:**
```
Estimated Value = Average Comparable PE × Target EPS
```

## Why PE Ratios Are Unreliable

The fundamental value of any financial instrument must be a function of:
1. Expected future cash flows
2. Timing of those cash flows
3. Riskiness of those cash flows

PE ratios measure none of these things directly. Earnings give a rough idea of future cash flows, but only approximately. As a result, PE ratios vary dramatically even among companies in the same industry.

**Hotel industry example (from lecture):**
| Company | PE Ratio |
|---|---|
| Hilton | ~36 |
| Marriott | ~24 |
| Choice Hotels | ~20 |
| Hyatt | ~79 |

These ratios are "all over the map." Which one would you use to value a new hotel chain?

## Key Takeaway

The PE model should only be used to get a "very rough ballpark figure." For rigorous valuation, use discounted cash flow analysis based on [[Free Cash Flow]] projections and [[Net Present Value]].
