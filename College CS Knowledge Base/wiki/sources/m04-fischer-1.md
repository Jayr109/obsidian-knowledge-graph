This transcript reviews the supply-and-demand model of real interest rate determination and motivates why nominal rates must be adjusted for inflation. It sets up the derivation in [[M4: Fischer Equation — Part 2]].

## Supply and Demand for Loanable Funds

The real interest rate is set by the intersection of:
- **Supply curve (upward sloping):** savings supplied is an increasing function of the real rate; shifts left as population ages or savings habits change.
- **Demand curve (downward sloping):** demand for loans decreases as rates rise; shifts with business investment appetite.

The equilibrium rate from this model is always the **real interest rate** — the rate at which your purchasing power actually grows.

## Real vs. Nominal Rates

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Real interest rate | Growth rate of purchasing power; what quoted rate would be with zero inflation |
| Nominal interest rate | The rate actually quoted (e.g., bank savings rate, bond coupon) |

The only rate you can observe directly is the nominal rate. To know how much your purchasing power actually grows, you must adjust for expected inflation.

## Motivation for Fischer Equation

Suppose the bank quotes 10% on CDs. If inflation is 7%, your $100 becomes $110 nominally — but that $110 buys less than $103 at today's prices. You need to adjust for inflation to find the true (real) return. The exact formula is derived in Part 2.
