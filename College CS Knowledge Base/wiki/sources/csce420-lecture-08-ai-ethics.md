## The Diagnosis

The deck's answer to *why AI ethics* is more specific than the usual framing:

> AI can possibly cause physical and/or mental harm and potentially cause societal disruption. **Part of the reason is: we do not understand fully why it works so well, and do not know how to fully control it.**

This ties the ethical problem directly to the technical one — the explainability limitation listed in [[Deep Learning]] is *why* accountability is hard, rather than a separate concern.

## The Sharpest Points

**"Exacerbates racism by standardizing people"** — listed among the negatives, and a more precise claim than "bias": the harm is in imposing a single statistical norm, not merely in imperfect data.

**Privacy is a property of the model.** *Training data can be extracted from the trained model through reverse engineering* — so a model can leak without any breach of a database.

**The useless class.** Many jobs will disappear; **even creativity, thought uniquely human, is in peril**; without retraining people become *not just unemployed but unemployable*.

**Augmentation vs. automation** (Brynjolfsson, *Daedalus* 2022) is the deck's constructive response, and the argument is economic: *"When AI is focused on augmenting humans rather than mimicking them, humans retain the power to insist on a share of the value created."* Mimicry makes humans substitutable; augmentation makes them complementary.

**Conscious AI and the cephalopod precedent.** If AI became conscious, should it get rights? The analogy is animal research — vertebrates strictly regulated, **cephalopods beginning to be** (EU Directive 2010/63/EU) — a real precedent for extending protection as evidence of sentience accumulates.

## Named Frameworks

OECD AI Principles; the US Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights; the EU Artificial Intelligence Act; Partnership on AI. The *Pause Giant AI Experiments* open letter is discussed against the precedent of the **Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA (1975)**.

Research-ethics material is credited to David Touretzky (CMU); the nondiscrimination discussion points to TAMU system policy.

## Note on the Slides

Slide 2 opens with portraits of Bentham, Mill, Kant, Dewey, and **Gebru** — placing Timnit Gebru alongside the canonical moral philosophers, which is itself the deck's argument that AI ethics belongs to ethics proper. The slide quotes both Britannica and **ChatGPT** on the definition of ethics, a deliberate touch in a deck about AI.
