This transcript argues that the [[Net Present Value]] rule is the only capital budgeting decision rule that directly measures shareholder value creation, and critiques the IRR, payback period, and profitability index as inferior alternatives.

## The Core Argument

**NPV is not just "the purest" rule (the textbook's word) — it is the only correct one.** NPV directly estimates how much a project increases or decreases the value of a business. All other rules measure something else.

## IRR vs. NPV

- **IRR** measures the *rate of return* on a project.
- **NPV** measures the *net value created* by a project.

These are related but not the same. When comparing mutually exclusive projects, they can conflict:

| Situation | IRR says | NPV says | Correct choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big low-return vs. small high-return | Small project | Big project | NPV (maximize value) |
| Short-term vs. long-term | Short-term | Long-term | NPV (maximize value) |

**Example:** A real estate developer choosing between a shopping district and an apartment complex on one parcel. The shopping district (bigger investment) may have lower IRR but higher NPV — the developer should build the shopping district.

## Payback Period

The payback period has no redeeming qualities as a decision rule: it ignores the time value of money entirely, and it does not measure value created or rate of return. Financial managers who use it "do not know what they are doing." The modified payback period is only marginally better.

## Profitability Index

The profitability index is an NPV adaptation for capital-constrained settings (when you cannot fund all positive-NPV projects). It ranks projects by NPV per dollar invested to identify the best feasible combination. However, it does not always give the correct answer — the only rigorous approach is to enumerate all feasible combinations and pick the one with maximum total NPV. Use the profitability index only as a quick back-of-envelope method when full enumeration is impractical.

## Bottom Line

"If you can, always use the NPV rule. That's because the NPV rule directly measures the degree to which an investment increases the value of a business."
