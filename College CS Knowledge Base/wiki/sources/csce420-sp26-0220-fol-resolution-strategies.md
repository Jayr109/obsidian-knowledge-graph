## Set Notation and Why It Helps

A clause is a **set of literals**; disjunction is union; and — the practical payoff — *"you can use set difference to get rid of some element."* The binary resolvent is (C₁σ − L₁σ) ∪ (C₂σ − L₂σ): apply the unifier σ of L₁ and ¬L₂ to **both** clauses, subtract the unified complementary literals, take the union. For {P(x), Q(x)} and {¬P(a), R(y)} with σ = {x/a}: {Q(a)} ∪ {R(y)} = Q(a) ∨ R(y). See [[Resolvents and Factors]].

**Factor-then-resolve example**: C₁ = P(x) ∨ P(f(y)) ∨ R(g(y)); C₂ = ¬P(f(g(a))) ∨ Q(b). Factor C₁ with {x/f(y)} → P(f(y)) ∨ R(g(y)). Unify P(f(y)) with P(f(g(a))): disagreement {y, g(a)} → {y/g(a)}, **applied to the entire factored clause** → R(g(g(a))) ∨ Q(b).

## Sound, Complete — and Checking Your Own KB

If S is unsatisfiable, resolution eventually derives False (complete); if False is derived, S was unsatisfiable (sound).

The weakness, with two cases: a KB containing False outright (*"really silly"*), or one containing P(x) and ¬P(a) — *"none of these individual clauses is false, but without even adding the negated conclusion, when you resolve these two you get False, and it'll invalidate anything."*

**The practical trick**: *"Just by running a theorem prover on the knowledge base itself — without any negated conclusion — it'll derive False if there's an inconsistency. You can use your theorem prover to check the integrity of your knowledge base."* See [[Resolution]].

## The Customs-Officials Theorem — Worked in Full

Not worked on the slides. Predicates: C(x) customs official, E(x) entered the country, V(x) VIP, **S(x, y) = x was searched by y** (*"whenever you have multi-argument predicates, make sure of the semantics — which direction"*), D(x) drug dealer.

**Translation points**:
- Premise 1: ∀x (E(x) ∧ ¬V(x) → ∃y (S(x,y) ∧ C(y))). The x inside the ∃ is *the same* x — variables link predicates across scopes.
- Premise 2, *"only searched by drug dealers"*: ∃x (E(x) ∧ D(x) ∧ ∀y (S(x,y) → D(y))). *"This 'only' is really what makes the ∀y necessary."*
- Premise 3, *"no drug dealer was a VIP"*: the word-by-word ¬∃x (D(x) ∧ V(x)) and the slide's ∀x (D(x) → ¬V(x)) are **equivalent** — push the negation in, De Morgan, then read ¬D ∨ ¬V as an implication.
- Conclusion: ∃x (D(x) ∧ C(x)); negated → ¬D(x) ∨ ¬C(x).

**Clauses (7)**. Premise 1 → two clauses after skolemizing y as **f(x)** and distributing: (1a) ¬E(x) ∨ V(x) ∨ S(x, f(x)); (1b) ¬E(x) ∨ V(x) ∨ C(f(x)). Premise 2 → **three** clauses, x skolemized to the constant **a**: (2a) E(a); (2b) D(a); (2c) ¬S(a, y) ∨ D(y). (3) ¬D(x) ∨ ¬V(x). (4) ¬D(x) ∨ ¬C(x). *"Whenever you have an ∧, these become separate lines."*

**His derivation**:

```
5.  1b + 2a  {x/a}      →  V(a) ∨ C(f(a))
6.  1a + 2a  {x/a}      →  V(a) ∨ S(a, f(a))
7.  6  + 2c  {y/f(a)}   →  V(a) ∨ D(f(a))
8.  2b + 3   {x/a}      →  ¬V(a)
9.  7  + 8               →  D(f(a))
10. 5  + 8               →  C(f(a))
11. 10 + 4   {x/f(a)}   →  ¬D(f(a))
12. 9  + 11              →  False
```

**Two remarks he made along the way.** First, both D(a) and D(f(a)) appear: *"a is a drug dealer, and f(a) is a drug dealer — f(a) is whoever searched a. We don't know who these are; they're Skolem constants and functions."* Second, **order matters in practice**: *"If you resolve some of these in a different order, you can get stuck"* — e.g. deriving ¬S(a, a) style dead ends that never cancel. Completeness guarantees a proof exists, not that any path finds it quickly.

## Implementation: Level Saturation

Sₙ = all resolvents with C₁ from S₀ ∪ … ∪ Sₙ₋₁ and **C₂ from Sₙ₋₁ only** — *"C₂ comes from only the newly generated ones."* He drew it as bands: old clauses on top, each new band generated by pairing everything above with the previous band. *"This is basically a breadth-first search"* — inefficient because irrelevant derivations pile up.

**Speed-ups**: delete tautologies (P(a) ∨ ¬P(a) ∨ … is True, *"adds no information"*), duplicates, and subsumed clauses (*"we're not going to worry too much about this"*). **Unit preference**: shortest clauses first — *"as you get toward the end the clauses get shorter: 3, 2, 1, then False has zero literals."* **Set of support**: start C₂ from the negated-conclusion clauses — *"this is exactly what's being done in level saturation"* if you begin the C₂ pointer at the goal.
