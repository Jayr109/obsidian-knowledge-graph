The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) states that financial market prices reflect all available information. It has three increasingly strong forms, each empirically contested to different degrees, and the 2022 Twitter acquisition case provides a vivid real-world test.

## Three Forms

### Strong Form
All information — public *and* private (including insider information and concealed fraud) — is reflected in prices.

**Status:** Rejected by evidence. Enron's massive accounting fraud was not priced in before exposure; insider trading prosecutions demonstrate that private information moves prices only when people trade on it.

### Semi-Strong Form
All *publicly available* information is reflected in prices. Prices adjust instantly when new public information arrives. You cannot earn excess returns by analyzing public news, filings, or announcements.

**Status:** Contested. The Twitter case (see [[M8: Twitter Acquisition — Part 2]]) provides a compelling apparent counterexample: even after a *publicly announced binding acquisition agreement*, the stock traded $5 below the stated deal price for months.

### Weak Form
All information in *past prices and volume* is reflected in current prices. Technical analysis (chart patterns, momentum strategies) cannot predict future returns beyond systematic risk compensation.

**Status:** Largely accepted. Note that using past prices to estimate beta — and expecting higher-beta stocks to earn higher *average* returns — does NOT violate weak-form efficiency; that is risk compensation, not excess return.

## The Twitter Case as a Test

In 2022, Elon Musk signed a binding agreement to buy Twitter for $54.20/share. Despite this public information, Twitter stock traded as low as $37 for months, seemingly presenting a near-risk-free arbitrage opportunity. See [[M8: Twitter Acquisition — Part 2]] for the full narrative and arguments on both sides.

**Case against semi-strong efficiency:** The price persistently failed to reflect the publicly known deal terms, even after legal commentary confirmed Twitter's case was a "slam dunk."

**Case for semi-strong efficiency:** Sophisticated arbitrageurs may have rationally priced genuine legal uncertainty that the instructor underweighted. The market may have been pricing real tail risks even if the instructor couldn't see them.

## Implications for Investors

- Under semi-strong EMH: reading earnings reports, M&A filings, and news adds no edge.
- Under weak-form EMH: technical analysis is worthless beyond risk-beta relationships.
- Neither form prevents outperformance via private information (though insider trading is illegal).
