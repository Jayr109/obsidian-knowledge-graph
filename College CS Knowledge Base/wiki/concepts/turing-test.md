The Turing Test, introduced by Alan Turing as the "imitation game" in *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* (Mind, LIX(236), 1950), replaces the unanswerable question "can machines think?" with an operational one: can a machine's conversational behavior be distinguished from a human's by an interrogator? It is the canonical move from unobservable inner states to observable behavior.

## Why It Exists

The [[Strong AI and Weak AI]] debate stalls on the **other minds problem** — there is no external test for consciousness. Turing's response is to decline the question. If a machine's responses are indistinguishable from a human's, he argues, insisting it still does not *really* think demands a standard we never apply to other people.

## Position in the Course

Lecture 1 lists the Turing Test under [[Foundations of AI]] → Mathematics, alongside Gödel's incompleteness theorem and the halting problem, because Turing's contributions to AI and to computability are the same intellectual project: formalizing what a machine can do. The lecture's framing phrase is the **computer/software metaphor** — representation/interpretation and symbol/computing as a model of mind.

The [[Chinese Room]] is the standard rebuttal, and it targets precisely the behavior-for-thought substitution the test performs.
