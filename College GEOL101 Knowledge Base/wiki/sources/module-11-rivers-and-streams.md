Module 11 covers running water: how streams collect and move water, how they erode, transport, and deposit sediment, the landforms they build, the hazards of flooding, and the surface-water issues facing society. Topics include drainage basins, discharge (Q = A × v), stream load, meandering vs. braided channels, deltas and alluvial fans, flood types and control, wetlands, and water pollution. Floods are the **most common and most costly natural disaster** in the world.

## Section 1: Hydrologic Stream Flow

- **Stream** — any body of water flowing in a channel. **River** — a larger stream fed by smaller ones.
- **Drainage basin (watershed)** — the region drained by a single river system. A **drainage divide** separates adjacent basins. A **drainage network** is the interconnected set of streams that collects water over an area.
- **Slope (gradient)** — vertical drop of the channel over horizontal distance. **Base level** — the theoretical lowest level to which a river can erode (ultimately sea level).
- Streams are fed by **snowmelt/rainfall in the headwaters** and by **groundwater** (which sustains flow between storms; **ephemeral streams** don't intersect the water table and don't flow year-round).
- **Case history — Pakistan Floods, August 2010**: record monsoon rains killed ~1,600 people, impacted 20 million, and flooded ~20% of the country. U.S. flooding averages >$2 billion in damage and ~100 deaths per year.

## Section 2: River Systems and Drainage Patterns

A river system has three zones: **sediment production** (erosion-dominated headwaters), **sediment transport** (trunk streams), and **sediment deposition** (mouth). In balance, sediment eroded equals sediment deposited.

**Drainage patterns** reflect the underlying geology:

| Pattern | Forms on |
|---------|----------|
| **Dendritic** (treelike) | Uniform underlying material — most common |
| **Radial** | Volcanic cones or domes |
| **Rectangular** | Jointed or faulted bedrock |
| **Trellis** | Alternating bands of resistant and weak strata |

## Section 3: Streamflow and Discharge

- Flow is **laminar** (smooth, straight) or **turbulent** (erratic, both horizontal and vertical motion); streams are typically turbulent.
- **Velocity** increases with steeper **gradient**, a more efficient channel shape (small **wetted perimeter** relative to cross-sectional area — narrow, deep channels flow more efficiently), and larger, smoother channels; boulders and woody debris add roughness and slow flow.
- **Discharge (Q)** — volume of water passing a location per unit time: **Q = A × v** (A = cross-sectional area, v = velocity), in m³/s or ft³/s. Controlled by channel width, channel depth, and flow velocity. *(Example: A = 200 ft² × 6 ft/s = 1,200 ft³/s; A = 800 ft² × 6 ft/s = 4,800 ft³/s.)*
- A **longitudinal profile** is the cross-sectional view from **head** (source) to **mouth**. It is typically **concave**: downstream, discharge, channel size, and velocity **increase** while slope **decreases**.

## Section 4: Stream Erosion and Sediment Transport

- **Erosion** acts by **abrasion** (sediment scraping/polishing the bed — forms **potholes** where swirling eddies drill the streambed), **hydraulic action** (force of moving water), and **corrosion/dissolution** (in limestone). It occurs as **downcutting** (vertical) and **lateral erosion** (on outer bends/cut banks).
- **Sediment load** is carried three ways: **dissolved load** (in solution, mostly from groundwater; unaffected by velocity), **suspended load** (the largest part — fine sand, silt, clay; controlled by velocity and settling velocity), and **bed load** (sand, gravel, boulders moving by **saltation**, sliding, or rolling; only at high flow).
- **Capacity** = maximum total load a stream can carry (increases with discharge). **Competence** = maximum particle size it can move (increases with velocity).
- **Deposition** occurs when velocity drops below settling velocity. Particles settle by size (**sorting**, largest first); stream-deposited sediment is **alluvium**.

## Section 5: Channel Types and Stream Valleys

- **Meandering channels** (one main channel, low gradient, fine load): the outer bend is a **cut bank** (erosion); the inner bend is a **point bar** (deposition). Bends migrate and can be cut off to form an **oxbow lake**. Where base level drops or land uplifts, **incised (entrenched) meanders** cut deep canyons (e.g., Goosenecks of the San Juan River, Utah — uplift of the Colorado Plateau).
- **Braided channels** (no main channel, high gradient, coarse load, variable discharge): a maze of channels and gravel bars; common at the toe of a glacier.
- **Bedrock channels** are cut into solid rock in steep headwaters, with **steps and pools**.
- **Stream valleys**: alluvial channels have wide flat floors; bedrock channels carve narrow **V-shaped valleys** (and **slot canyons** in arid climates). **Valley deepening** by downcutting makes V-shaped valleys with rapids and waterfalls; **valley widening** by migrating meanders produces a **floodplain**.

## Section 6: Depositional Landforms

- **Floodplain** — flat valley floor covered during floods by shifting meanders.
- **Natural levees** — raised banks built when overbank flood water suddenly loses velocity and drops coarse sediment beside the channel; poorly drained **back swamps** and **yazoo tributaries** form behind them.
- **Deltas** — form where a sediment-laden stream enters still water; coarse **foreset beds** near the mouth, fine **bottomset beds** at the edge; the channel splits into **distributaries**. The **Mississippi River Delta** is seven coalescing deltas; today's **bird-foot delta** formed over ~500 years, and the river is trying to switch to the Atchafalaya (held in place by engineering).
- **Alluvial fans** — form where a high-gradient stream leaves a narrow mountain valley and abruptly drops gradient; coarse material near the apex, fine toward the base; common in arid climates (e.g., Death Valley).

## Section 7: Floods and Flood Control

- **Flooding** — overbank flow where discharge exceeds the channel's capacity. **Stage** = water height at a location; **lag time** = delay between peak rainfall and peak flooding; a **hydrograph** graphs discharge over time.
- **Flood types**: **regional** (seasonal — rain on snowpack/frozen or saturated ground; e.g., Mississippi 1993), **flash** (high-intensity rain funneled into narrow channels — mountains, deserts, urban areas; 50% of flash-flood deaths occur in vehicles), and **storm-surge** (onshore hurricane winds + low pressure push water onto land; cause 9 of 10 hurricane deaths; Galveston 1900 killed 8,000–12,000).
- **Recurrence interval**: a **"100-year flood"** has a 1% probability in any given year (a 25-year event is smaller but four times as likely). Needs 20–30 years of stream-gage data.
- **Case studies**: Big Thompson Canyon, CO (1976; stationary storm dropped >12 in, 144 deaths); Death Valley flash floods (2015; $47M damage to Scotty's Castle); Zion/Keyhole Canyon, UT (2015; 20 killed).
- **Human causes**: urbanization (impervious surfaces, storm sewers), wetland destruction, channelization, tile drains, farmland snowmelt.
- **Flood control**: dams/reservoirs, channelization (floodways), levees, floodwalls — plus nonstructural measures (relocation, floodplain land-use limits, **wetland restoration**, the **National Flood Insurance** program, building codes). **Dam failures** can be catastrophic: Johnstown, PA (1889; 2,209 deaths), Teton Dam, ID (1976; 11 deaths), St. Francis Dam, CA (1928).

## Section 8: Surface-Water Issues, Wetlands, and Pollution

- **Surface-water issues**: land-use change (urbanization, deforestation), contamination, dam construction/failure, wetland destruction, and over-use (especially in deserts). Forest→farmland increases erosion and gradient; urban build-up increases impervious cover, raising flood frequency and shortening lag time.
- **Wetlands** ("nature's filters") require **hydrology** (saturated soils in the growing season), **hydrophytic vegetation** (cattails, sawgrass, willows), and **hydric soils** (anaerobic). Two types: coastal (5%) and freshwater (95%). They filter contaminants, buffer storm surge, slow runoff, and provide habitat. The U.S. has lost ~55% of its wetlands since the 1600s; Ohio's drained **Great Black Swamp** once filtered water into Lake Erie's western basin — its loss contributes to today's **harmful algal blooms (HABs)**.
- **Water pollution** is detailed on [[Water Pollution and Contamination]]: HABs (Lake Erie/Toledo 2014, Florida red tides), the Cuyahoga River fire (1969 → Clean Water Act 1970), acid mine drainage, and federal water laws.
