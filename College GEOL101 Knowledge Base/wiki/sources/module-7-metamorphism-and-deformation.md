Module 7 covers the formation and classification of metamorphic rocks, the processes and agents that drive metamorphism, metamorphic textures and environments, rock deformation (stress/strain, brittle vs. ductile), fault and fold types, and mountain-building mechanisms (orogenesis). Instructor: Dr. Brandi Lenz. Source PDF: 156 pages (includes both Metamorphic Rocks and Structural Geology/Mountain Building lecture sections).

## Topics Covered

### What Are Metamorphic Rocks?
- Formed when **parent rocks (protoliths)** are subjected to intense heat and/or pressure, causing changes in mineralogy and sometimes chemical composition.
- The rock remains **essentially solid** during metamorphism; if melting occurs, igneous activity begins.
- Classified based on grain alignment: **Foliated** (aligned minerals) or **Non-foliated** (no preferred orientation).

### Metamorphic Grade
- **Metamorphic grade** = degree to which the parent rock changes.
- Ranges from **low grade** (low temperature/pressure) → **high grade** (high temperature/pressure).
- Textural progression: Slate → Phyllite → Schist → Gneiss (low to high grade).

### Metamorphic Processes
- **Recrystallization** – minerals change in size and/or shape, altering the rock's texture.
  - *Pressure solution*: minerals partially dissolve then recrystallize.
  - *Plastic deformation*: mineral grains soften and deform into aligned orientations (produces foliation).
- **Neocrystallization** – original minerals become unstable and change into new minerals.
  - Can create **polymorphs**: same chemical composition, different crystalline structure (e.g., diamond vs. graphite; Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs).
  - Can change chemical composition via reactions similar to chemical weathering.

### Agents (Causes) of Metamorphism
1. **Heat** — most important agent; provides energy for chemical reactions.
   - Sources: geothermal gradient (~25°C/km depth) and contact with rising magma.
2. **Confining pressure** — force applied equally in all directions; compacts rocks, increases density, does not cause deformation alone.
3. **Differential stress** — unequal forces in different directions; drives deformation.
   - *Compressional stress*: squeezes/shortens in one direction, elongates in another; rocks are ductile at high P/T.
4. **Chemically active fluids** — hot, ion-rich hydrothermal solutions enhance ion migration, aid recrystallization and neocrystallization, and can change overall chemistry.
- **Parent rock composition** matters: most metamorphic rocks retain the same bulk chemistry as the protolith (except loss/gain of volatiles: H₂O, CO₂).

### Metamorphic Textures

#### Foliation
**Foliation** = any planar arrangement of mineral grains within a rock. Forms by:
- Rotation of platy minerals.
- Recrystallization.
- Flattening of spherical grains.

Examples: parallel alignment of platy/elongated minerals, compositional banding of light/dark minerals, cleavage allowing easy splitting.

| Texture | Description | Example Rock |
|---|---|---|
| Rock/slaty cleavage | Rocks split into thin slabs; low-grade metamorphism of shale | Slate |
| Schistosity | Platy minerals (mica, chlorite) visible to naked eye; planar/layered | Schist |
| Gneissic texture | High-grade; ion migration segregates light and dark mineral bands | Gneiss |

#### Non-Foliated Textures
- Form where **compressional stress is minimal**.
- Parent rock develops equidimensional crystals.
- **Porphyroblastic texture**: unusually large grains (**porphyroblasts**) surrounded by fine-grained matrix.

### Common Metamorphic Rocks

#### Foliated
| Rock | Grain Size | Protolith | Grade | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slate | Very fine | Shale / mudstone / siltstone | Low | Splits into thin slabs; rock cleavage |
| Phyllite | Fine (not naked-eye) | Shale | Low–medium | Glossy sheen, wavy surfaces; rock cleavage |
| Schist | Medium–coarse | Shale | Medium–high | Platy minerals (micas) dominant; may have porphyroblasts |
| Gneiss | Medium–coarse | Granite (or high-grade schist) | High | Banded light/dark layers; does not split as easily |

#### Non-Foliated
| Rock | Protolith | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Marble | Limestone or dolostone | Crystalline; main mineral calcite (Mohs 3); various colors from impurities; weathers in acid rain |
| Quartzite | Quartz sandstone | Quartz grains fused; pure quartzite is white |
| Hornfels | Shale / clay-rich rock | Fine-grained; variable minerals; "baked" by intruding magma |

### Metamorphic Environments

| Environment | Driver | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Contact (thermal) | Heat from magma | Upper crust; low P, high T; produces **aureoles** around intrusions |
| Hydrothermal | Hot ion-rich fluids | Mid-ocean ridges (black smokers), hot springs; chemical alteration |
| Burial | Confining P + heat | Thick sedimentary basins subsiding |
| Subduction zone | Pressure before temperature | Subducting slab; differential stress dominant |
| Regional | Heat + P + differential stress | Mountain-building zones; most common; largest volumes of metamorphic rock |
| Fault zone | Ductile flow at depth | Produces **mylonites** (foliated/lineated rocks) |
| Impact (shock) | Meteoroid impact | Produces **impactites** (fused fragments + glass-rich ejecta) |

## Stress, Strain, and Rock Deformation

### Stress Types (Force/Area)
- **Confining pressure**: equal in all directions; compacts but does not deform.
- **Differential stress**: unequal forces.
  - *Compressional*: squeezes rock → convergent boundaries.
  - *Tensional*: pulls rock apart → divergent boundaries.
  - *Shear*: slides one block past another → transform boundaries.

### Strain Types (Response to Stress)
- **Elastic deformation**: temporary; rock returns to original shape when stress removed.
- **Brittle deformation**: permanent breaks (faults, joints); surface/shallow conditions.
- **Ductile deformation**: permanent change in shape without breaking (folds); deep/hot conditions.

### Factors Affecting Deformation
1. **Temperature** — high T → ductile; near surface → brittle.
2. **Confining pressure** — higher P → rock more likely to bend than fracture.
3. **Rock type** — igneous and some metamorphic rocks tend to be brittle; weakly cemented sedimentary rocks more ductile.
4. **Time** — given enough time, rocks accommodate tens of km of compressional strain by ductile flow.

## Faults (Brittle Deformation)

A **fault** is a fracture in crustal rock with displacement on one or both sides.

### Terminology
- **Hanging wall**: rock above the fault plane.
- **Footwall**: rock below the fault plane.
- **Fault scarp**: low cliff produced by vertical displacement.
- **Slickensides**: polished, striated fault surfaces formed as blocks slide past each other.
- **Joints**: fractures with no appreciable displacement; one of the most common rock structures.

### Fault Types

| Type | Motion | Stress | Plate Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal fault | Hanging wall drops relative to footwall | Tension | Divergent |
| Reverse fault | Hanging wall rises relative to footwall | Compression | Convergent |
| Thrust fault | Low-angle reverse fault | Compression | Convergent |
| Strike-slip fault | Horizontal motion along fault plane | Shear | Transform |
| Oblique-slip | Both strike-slip and dip-slip motion | Mixed | Mixed |

- **Horst and Graben**: system of upthrown (horst) and downdropped (graben) blocks along normal faults.

## Folds (Ductile Deformation)

**Folds** are wave-like undulations formed mainly by compressional stress → lateral shortening + vertical thickening.

### Terminology
- **Hinge line**: imaginary axis around which each layer is bent (can be horizontal or inclined).
- **Axial plane**: surface connecting all hinge lines.
- **Limbs**: the sides of a fold.

### Fold Types

| Type | Description | Age Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Anticline | Upfolded/arched layers | Oldest in center |
| Syncline | Downfolded trough | Youngest in center |
| Symmetrical | Limbs are mirror images | — |
| Asymmetrical | Limbs are not identical | — |
| Overturned (recumbent) | One or both limbs tilted past vertical | — |
| Plunging | Fold axis penetrates the ground | — |
| Dome | Broad upwarp; circular/elongated bulge | Oldest in center |
| Basin | Downwarped circular feature | Youngest in center |
| Monocline | Large steplike fold in otherwise horizontal strata; coupled with faults | — |

## Mountain Building (Orogenesis)

**Orogenesis** = the process that collectively produces a mountain belt; an episode is called an **orogeny**.

### Subduction Zone Mountain Building

**Volcanic Island Arc** (oceanic–oceanic subduction):
- Subducting slab melts overlying mantle wedge → magma rises → island arc.
- Example: Japan.

**Andean-Type** (oceanic–continental subduction):
- Long-lasting magmatic activity + crustal thickening.
- Features: volcanic arc, deep-ocean trench, forearc region, back-arc region.
- Volatiles from descending crust trigger partial melting of peridotite → mafic primary magmas.
- Thick crust impedes magma ascent → crystallizes as **batholiths** (diorite to granite). Example: Sierra Nevada.
- **Accretionary wedge**: deformed/thrust-faulted sediments scraped off subducting plate.
- **Forearc basin**: sediments trapped between accretionary wedge and volcanic arc.
- **Back-arc spreading**: cold subducting plate sinks vertically → trench rolls back → overlying plate stretches → possible seafloor spreading.
- Example: Andes Mountains; California (Sierra Nevada, Coast Ranges, Great Valley) formed by Farallon Plate subduction.

### Collisional Mountain Building

**Alpine-Type** (continent–continent collision):
- Two continents collide at a **suture zone** (contains slivers of oceanic lithosphere and accreted terrane).
- Produces **fold-and-thrust belts**.
- Example: **Himalayas** — India–Asia collision began 30–50 Ma; India still moving north; escape tectonics displaces Asian crust laterally.

**The Appalachians** — three orogenies during Pangaea assembly:
1. **Taconic Orogeny** (~450 Ma): volcanic arc thrust over eastern North America.
2. **Acadian Orogeny** (~350 Ma): micro-continent collides with North America; thrust faults, metamorphism, granite intrusions.
3. **Alleghanian Orogeny** (~250–300 Ma): Africa collides with North America; Pangaea rifts 180 Ma leaving piece of Africa welded to North America.

**Cordilleran-Type** (terrane accretion):
- **Terranes**: distinct accreted blocks of crust (former microcontinents or island arcs).
- Small/dense features subduct; large/buoyant features collide and accrete.
- North American Cordillera assembled from terranes during Farallon Plate subduction after Pangaea breakup.

### Fault-Block Mountains
- Form from **continental rifting** (tensional forces).
- **Basin and Range Province**: largest region of fault-block mountains on Earth; between Sierra Nevada and Rockies; encompasses Nevada and surrounding states.
  - Two formation theories: (1) Pacific plate northwest movement after Farallon subduction produced tension; (2) lithospheric delamination ~20 Ma → hot mantle upwelling + lateral spreading.
