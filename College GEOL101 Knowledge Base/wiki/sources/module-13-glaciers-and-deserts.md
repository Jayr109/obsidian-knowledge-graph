Module 13 covers two contrasting climate-driven landscapes and the geologic work that shapes them: **glaciers** (moving masses of ice) and **deserts** (dry lands shaped by infrequent water and persistent wind). It spans how glaciers form, move, erode, and deposit; the imprint of the ice ages; why dry climates exist; weathering and running water in arid lands; and wind (eolian) erosion and deposition.

## Section 1: Glaciers and Glacial Movement
- A **glacier** is a thick mass of ice formed over centuries by the accumulation, compaction, and recrystallization of snow; it is part of both the **hydrologic cycle** and the **rock cycle**.
- Types: **valley (alpine) glaciers**, continental **ice sheets** (Greenland, Antarctica), **ice caps**, **outlet glaciers**, **piedmont glaciers**, and floating **ice shelves** (fed by tidewater glaciers). If Antarctica's ice melted, sea level would rise ~70 m.
- Formation: snow → granular snow → **firn** → interlocking glacial ice.
- Movement: **plastic flow** (internal) plus **basal slip** (sliding on meltwater). The brittle upper ~50 m is the **zone of fracture**, where **crevasses** open. Flow is fastest at the center, dragged at the sides and base.
- **Glacial budget**: the **zone of accumulation** (above the snowline) vs. the **zone of wastage**, where ice is lost by **ablation** (melting + **calving** of icebergs). Accumulation > loss → the front advances; loss > accumulation → it retreats. Most glaciers are retreating today.

## Section 2: Glacial Erosion and Deposition
- Erosion by **plucking** (meltwater freezes and lifts blocks) and **abrasion** (producing **rock flour** and **striations**).
- Erosional landforms: **U-shaped troughs**, truncated spurs, **hanging valleys**, **cirques** (with **tarns** and paternoster lakes), **cols**, **arêtes**, **horns**, **roches moutonnées**, and **fjords** (drowned troughs).
- Deposits: **glacial drift** = all glacial sediment. **Till** is unsorted, ice-deposited material (with **glacial erratics**); **stratified drift** is sorted meltwater sediment.
- Till landforms: lateral, **medial** (two glaciers merge), **end (terminal)**, and ground **moraines**, plus **drumlins** (streamlined till hills; steep side faces the ice source). Stratified-drift landforms: **outwash plains**/valley trains, **kettles**, **kames**, and **eskers**.

## Section 3: Ice Ages and Their Effects
- The Quaternary Ice Age spans the past ~2.6 million years with ~20 glacial/interglacial cycles (~100,000-year cycles); the **Last Glacial Maximum** was ~18,000 years ago.
- Effects: sea level ~100 m lower at the LGM; crustal **subsidence and isostatic rebound**; creation of the **Great Lakes** and **Finger Lakes**; rerouted rivers; **proglacial lakes** (Agassiz, Missoula — ice-dam failures caused megafloods); and **pluvial lakes** (Bonneville).
- Evidence relies on glacial deposits and **uniformitarianism**; **tillite** (lithified till) records earlier ice ages. Causes: **plate tectonics** (continents drifting poleward, ocean circulation), **Milankovitch orbital variations** (eccentricity, obliquity, precession), and changes in atmosphere/ocean/albedo.

## Section 4: Deserts and Dry Climates
- A **dry climate** is one where annual precipitation is less than potential evaporation; dry lands cover ~30% of Earth's surface, split into **desert (arid)** and **steppe (semiarid)** regions. **Desertification** is expanding.
- Causes of dry land: **subsiding air** (subtropical highs ~20–35°, the "horse latitudes" → Sahara), **cold ocean currents** (west-coast deserts → Atacama, Namib), **continental interiors** (Gobi), and **rain shadows** behind mountain barriers (Sierra Nevada, Cascades).
- Dry-region weathering is mostly **mechanical**; **desert varnish** (clay + Mn/Fe oxides) coats surfaces (and petroglyphs).
- Water still does most desert erosion: **ephemeral streams** (wash, arroyo, wadi) and flash floods; some **exotic** permanent rivers cross deserts (Colorado, Nile).
- Arid landscapes: interior drainage; **Basin and Range** (alluvial fans → **bajada** → **playa lake/playa**, **inselbergs**) and the **Colorado Plateau** (mesas, buttes, pinnacles).

## Section 5: Wind (Eolian) Erosion and Deposition
- Wind erosion is most effective in dry lands; unlike water, wind is low-density (moves only fine grains) and unconfined.
- Transport: **bed load** (sand by **saltation**, <1 m high) and **suspended load** (silt; Sahara dust reaches the Caribbean).
- Erosion: **deflation** (surface lowering; **blowouts**; the Dust Bowl) and **abrasion** (**ventifacts**, **yardangs**). **Desert pavement** armors the surface.
- Deposits: **dunes** (gentle windward slope, **slip face** at the 34° angle of repose; **cross-bedding**) in types — barchan, transverse, barchanoid, longitudinal, parabolic, star — and **loess** (windblown silt from deserts or glacial outwash).
