## Part 1: Mechanical Weathering (pages 1–19)

Weathering involves the physical breakdown and chemical alteration of rock at or near Earth's surface. Two types work simultaneously:
- **Mechanical weathering** (disintegration) — physical forces break rock into smaller pieces, increasing surface area.
- **Chemical weathering** (decomposition) — chemical transformation into new compounds.
- **Erosion** — removal and transport of weathered rock by water, wind, or ice.

Controls on weathering rate: physical nature of rock (hardness, fracturing), chemical nature (iron-bearing, soluble, carbonate vs. non-carbonate), and climate.

### Weathering Geometries
Granular Disintegration, Spheroidal Weathering, Exfoliation, Block Separation, Shattering.

### Types of Mechanical Weathering
- **Frost Wedging** — freeze/thaw of water in cracks; creates angular fragments; occurs at high latitude/elevation and tundra.
- **Salt Crystal Growth** — crystals grow in cracks (halite, gypsum) exerting pressure; occurs in arid environments; primary form: granular disintegration.
- **Sheeting/Unloading (Pressure Release)** — deeply buried rocks exhumed by erosion; stress release; most common in massive rocks (granite); primary forms: exfoliation, block separation.
- **Hydration** — wetting/drying; no chemical change; primary forms: shattering, block separation.
- **Biological Activity** — plant roots wedge rock apart; burrowing animals enhance weathering; decaying organisms produce acids.

## Part 2: Chemical Weathering (pages 21–57)

Chemical weathering breaks down rocks and mineral internal structures. Most important agent: water.

### Processes
- **Dissolution** — dissolving into solution; simple solution (halite); carbonation: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid) attacks calcite/limestone → caves; physical form: granular disintegration.
- **Oxidation** — metals (especially Fe) combine with oxygen; produces iron oxide colors; physical form: granular disintegration.
- **Hydrolysis** — chemical reaction between water and rock; most common in granite; byproduct: clay minerals; reaction: 2KAlSi₃O₈ + 2H⁺ + 9H₂O → Kaolinite + Silicic Acid + K⁺.

### Rates of Weathering
- Influenced by rock characteristics (mineral composition, solubility) and climate (temperature, precipitation).
- Silicate minerals weather in same order as Bowen's Reaction Series.
- **Differential weathering** — rock masses do not weather uniformly.

## Part 3: Soil (pages 34–57)

- **Regolith** — layer of rock and mineral fragments produced by weathering covering Earth's land surface.
- **Soil** — portion of regolith that supports plant growth; ~50% disintegrated rock + mineral matter + humus.
- **Humus** — organic matter from decomposition of plants and animals.

### Controls of Soil Formation
1. **Parent material** — source of weathered material; residual soils (from bedrock) vs. transported soils (from sediment); composition determines fertility.
2. **Climate** — most influential; temperature and precipitation control weathering rate and degree.
3. **Plants, Animals, and Microbes** — primary source of organic matter; bioturbation (earthworm mixing).
4. **Time** — longer weathering → thicker soil that resembles parent material less.
5. **Topography** — steep slopes → thin, poorly developed soils; flat/undulating → optimal; south-facing slopes (N. Hemisphere) optimal.

### Soil Profile (Horizons)
- **O horizon** — organic matter (plant matter and decomposed material); topsoil.
- **A horizon** — organic + mineral matter; high biological activity; topsoil.
- **E horizon** — little organic matter; light-colored; eluviation (fine components washed out); leaching.
- **B horizon (subsoil)** — zone of accumulation; receives material from E horizon.
- **C horizon** — partially altered parent material.

### Soil Erosion and Conservation
- Erosion rates depend on climate, slope, and vegetation.
- Deforestation/farming enhance erosion: sheet erosion → rills → gullies.
- Case study: 1930s Dust Bowl (Great Plains — OK, TX, KS); unprotected soils + drought.
- Conservation: terracing, strip cropping, grassed drainage ways.

## Part 4: Sedimentary Rocks — Overview (pages 59–72)

Sedimentary rocks form by accumulation and cementation (lithification) of sediments. Key terms:
- **Sediment** — loose particles from weathering/erosion or precipitation from solution.
- **Lithification** — consolidation of sediments; includes compaction, cementation, recrystallization.
- **Cement** — solid material precipitating in pore space.
- **Bedding/Stratification** — horizontal layering; bedding planes separate layers.
- **Fossils** — evidence of pre-existing life; whole organisms, tracks, trails, fecal material; used for dating and paleoenvironment reconstruction.

Three categories: **Clastic**, **Biological (Organic)**, **Chemical**.

Economic importance: fossil fuels (oil, gas, coal), minerals (uranium, iron ore, gypsum), sand, gravel, limestone (building stone), fertilizer (phosphates, nitrates), groundwater resources.

## Part 5: Clastic Sedimentary Rocks (pages 73–91)

Form from weathered and transported sediment fragments. Chief minerals: clay minerals, quartz, feldspars, micas. Classified by **particle size**.

| Rock | Particle Size | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------|
| Shale | Silt + clay (fine) | Most abundant sedimentary rock; fissility; quiet environments; crumbles easily; gentle slopes |
| Mudstone | Clay (fine) | Breaks into chunks; lacks fissility |
| Siltstone | Silt (fine) | Grouped with shale; less clay |
| Sandstone | Sand | Second most abundant; forms in varied environments; quartz dominant |
| Conglomerate | Gravel (rounded) | Poorly sorted; rounded clasts |
| Breccia | Gravel (angular) | Poorly sorted; angular clasts |

### Sandstone Varieties
- **Quartz sandstone** — predominantly quartz.
- **Arkose** — appreciable feldspar.
- **Graywacke** — rock fragments + matrix + quartz.

### Particle Sorting and Shape
- **Sorting** — degree of similarity in particle size; well-sorted (similar sizes) vs. poorly sorted (mixed sizes).
- **Rounding** — rounded = transported far; angular = transported short distance.
- Grain size decreases, roundness increases, and sorting increases with distance from source: Breccia → Conglomerate → Sandstone → Shale.

## Part 6: Organic (Biological) Sedimentary Rocks (pages 92–98)

Form from carbon-rich remains of organisms.
- **Coal** — from plant remains; forms in stagnant, oxygen-poor swamp environments. Four stages: plant accumulation → peat/lignite → bituminous coal → anthracite coal.
- **Coquina** (limestone) — cemented shell fragments.
- **Oil Shale** — from remains of algae + clay minerals; contains kerogen.

## Part 7: Chemical Sedimentary Rocks (pages 99–113)

Form from precipitated material once in solution; by inorganic processes (evaporation) or organic processes.

- **Limestone** — most abundant chemical sedimentary rock; mainly calcite; inorganic or biochemical origin.
  - *Biochemical*: from marine organism shells/coral reefs; coquina (shell fragments); chalk (microscopic marine organisms).
  - *Inorganic*: travertine (cave precipitation); oolitic limestone (ooids in shallow marine).
- **Dolostone** — similar to limestone but contains magnesium; forms when Mg-rich waters circulate through limestone.
- **Chert** — microcrystalline quartz; varieties include flint, jasper, agate.
- **Evaporites** — form in restricted seaways or salt flats; rock salt and rock gypsum; example: Bonneville Salt Flats.

Limestone is Earth's largest carbon repository (part of the carbon cycle).

## Part 8: Sedimentary Structures (pages 118–128)

- **Strata/Beds** — layers; most common sedimentary feature; separated by bedding planes.
- **Cross-bedding** — inclined layers; characteristic of sand dunes, deltas, stream deposits.
- **Graded beds** — coarse at bottom to fine at top; associated with turbidity currents.
- **Ripple marks** — small lithified waves; oscillation (symmetric) vs. current (asymmetric).
- **Mud cracks** — indicate alternately wet and dry environment.
- **Fossils** — evidence of prehistoric life.

## Part 9: Depositional Environments (pages 129–137)

A **sedimentary environment** is a geographic setting where sediment accumulates; determines grain size, shape, and composition.

Three broad categories:
- **Continental**: streams (dominant agent), glacial (unsorted clay to boulders), wind/eolian (well-sorted fine sediment).
- **Marine**: shallow marine (to ~200 m, terrestrial sediments or carbonate-rich muds near reefs); deep marine (fine sediments, turbidity current deposits).
- **Transitional**: shoreline zone — beaches, tidal flats, spits/bars/barrier islands, lagoons, deltas.

**Sedimentary facies** — different sediments depositing in adjacent environments simultaneously (e.g., sand on beach while mud deposits offshore); transitions are gradual.
