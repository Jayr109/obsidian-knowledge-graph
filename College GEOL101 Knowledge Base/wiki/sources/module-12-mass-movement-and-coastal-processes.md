Module 12 covers two families of gravity- and water-driven surface hazards: **mass movement** (the downslope movement of rock, regolith, and soil under gravity) and **coastal processes** (how waves, currents, and storms shape and threaten shorelines). It spans the classification of mass-wasting events, what makes slopes fail and how to stabilize them, wave and longshore dynamics, erosional and depositional coastal landforms, engineered shoreline protection, and hurricanes as the ultimate coastal hazard.

## Section 1: Introduction to Mass Movement
- **Mass movement (mass wasting)** — downslope movement of rock, regolith, soil, snow, and ice under the direct influence of **gravity**; it is the geologic step that often follows [[Weathering and Soil]] and, combined with running water, carves most stream valleys.
- A **landslide** is a general (non-technical) term for the rapid downslope movement of rock and soil. Hundreds die yearly; the deadliest in history (Ningxia, China, 1920) killed >100,000 in earthquake-triggered slides.
- **Vaiont (Vajont) Dam, Italy (1963)** — rock layers dipping toward the reservoir, weakened by rising water, sent ~240 million m³ of rock into the lake at 68 mph; a 230-ft wave overtopped the (still-standing) dam and killed >2,000 in Longarone.

## Section 2: Types of Mass Wasting
Classified by **material** (rock vs. soil/regolith) and **type of motion**, with speed increasing down the list:
- **Falls** — free-fall of rock (rock fall) from cliffs.
- **Slides** — coherent movement on a surface: **translational slides** (move along a flat plane of weakness) and **rotational slides / slumps** (curved surface, backward rotation, head scarp + lumpy toe).
- **Flows** — water-saturated viscous movement: **creep** (slowest — soil creep, rock creep; tilts poles/trees), **earthflow**, **debris flow**, **mudflow** (>50% water), **lahar** (volcanic mudflow), and **debris avalanche** (fastest). Nevados Huascarán, Peru (1970, earthquake-triggered) buried 22,000 people.
- **Subsidence** — settling from fluid withdrawal or collapse over voids; **snow avalanches** (~10,000/yr in the U.S.); and **underwater landslides** that can trigger or amplify tsunamis.

## Section 3: Slope Stability, Triggers, and Prevention
- **Driving forces** (weight, gravity) vs. **resisting forces** (shear strength, vegetation). The **angle of repose** is the steepest stable slope for loose sediment (~35° for dry sand).
- A little water adds **cohesion** (surface tension); too much water raises **pore pressure** and pushes grains apart. **Zones of weakness** — bedding, foliation, joints, faults — act as failure surfaces.
- **Triggers**: volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, heavy precipitation, and land-use change (oversteepening, cutting the slope toe, removing vegetation). **Prevention**: retaining walls, gabions, replanting, drainage, regrading, rock bolts/screens, and debris barriers.

## Section 4: Shorelines and Waves
- **Coast/shore/beach** definitions; **waves** form by wind friction and grow with wind **velocity, duration, and fetch**. Wave energy ∝ height²; **wave base** ≈ ½ wavelength. Waves shoal, steepen, and break in the **surf zone**.
- **Wave refraction** bends waves to focus energy on **headlands** and weaken it in **bays**, straightening coastlines. **Longshore currents** and **beach drift** move sediment ("rivers of sand"). **Rip currents** (~100 U.S. deaths/yr) flow seaward through sandbar gaps.

## Section 5: Coastal Landforms and Classification
- **Erosional**: wave-cut cliffs, wave-cut platforms, marine terraces (uplifted platforms), sea caves → **sea arches** → **sea stacks**.
- **Depositional**: beaches, **spits**, baymouth bars, **tombolos**, and **barrier islands** (Atlantic/Gulf coasts).
- **Emergent coasts** (uplift/sea-level fall → cliffs, terraces; California) vs. **submergent coasts** (subsidence/sea-level rise → estuaries, irregular shorelines; Atlantic Coast).

## Section 6: Coastal Hazards and Protection
- Hazards: hurricanes, nor'easters, **tidal flooding** (high tide + surge; king tides), tsunamis, and **coastal erosion** (~3.3 ft/yr on the Atlantic/Gulf).
- **Hard structures**: seawalls (parallel), groins (perpendicular; trap updrift, erode downdrift), jetties (paired at inlets), breakwaters (offshore) — all disrupt longshore transport and shift erosion elsewhere.
- **Soft/non-structural**: beach nourishment, dune protection, setback zones (E-60 line), and relocation (the Cape Hatteras Lighthouse was moved 2,900 ft in 1999).

## Section 7: Hurricanes
- Greatest storms on Earth (typhoons in the W Pacific, cyclones in the Indian Ocean). Form over **≥27 °C** water in late summer/fall, between ~5° and 20° latitude. Progression: tropical depression → **tropical storm** (≥39 mph) → **hurricane** (≥74 mph).
- Structure: low-pressure **eye** (calm), **eye wall** (strongest wind/rain), spiral bands; rotate counterclockwise (N Hemisphere). Ranked by the **Saffir-Simpson scale** (Cat 1–5).
- **Storm surge** causes the most deaths (worse on the right of the eye); plus wind, inland flooding, and tornadoes. Case studies: **Galveston 1900** (deadliest U.S. disaster, 8,000–12,000 dead), **Katrina 2005** (New Orleans levee failures, $108B), **Harvey 2017** (Houston rainfall flooding).
