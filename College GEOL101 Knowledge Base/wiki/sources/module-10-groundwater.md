Module 10 covers groundwater: where it comes from, how it is stored and moves through the subsurface, the landforms it creates, how humans use it as a resource, and how it becomes contaminated. Topics include the hydrologic cycle, porosity and permeability, the water table and subsurface zones, aquifers and aquitards, Darcy's law, wells and artesian systems, springs/hot springs/geysers, karst topography, the Ogallala Aquifer, subsidence, saltwater intrusion, and groundwater pollution with cleanup methods.

## Section 1: Introduction — Why Groundwater Matters

- Drinking water comes from **surface water** (streams and lakes) and **groundwater** (water in rocks or unconsolidated materials below the surface). Bottled water is largely repackaged tap water (~¼ of bottled water) and costs ~1,000× more.
- Of all water on Earth, **less than 1% is usable for life**; over **68% of freshwater is locked in ice caps and glaciers**. Of the non-frozen freshwater, **over 96% is groundwater**.
- The U.S. uses ~**306 billion gallons of freshwater per day**; ~**25% comes from groundwater**, used primarily for irrigation.
- Geological importance of groundwater: an **erosional agent** (dissolves soluble bedrock like limestone → caves, sinkholes) and an **equalizer of stream flow** (sustains streams during dry periods).
- The ultimate source of all groundwater is **precipitation**.

### Runoff vs. Infiltration
The amount of water entering the groundwater system is a "battle" between **surface runoff (overland flow)** and **infiltration**. Overland flow occurs when precipitation rate exceeds infiltration rate. Factors affecting runoff/infiltration: geologic (rock/soil type, weathering, structure), physiographic (basin shape, relief, slope), climatic (precipitation type/intensity/duration), and biologic (vegetation, animals). Steeper slopes, low porosity/permeability, high-intensity rain, sparse vegetation, and urbanization all **increase runoff and decrease infiltration**.

## Section 2: Porosity and Permeability

- **Porosity** — percentage of empty (pore) space in a rock or soil; determines how much water can be stored. Depends on grain size/shape, sorting, and packing. Poorly sorted sediments have low porosity; most crystalline rocks gain porosity only through fractures.
- **Permeability** — the ability of a material to transmit fluids; depends on how well pores are connected. Determines how fast water moves. Water follows a tortuous path from pore to pore.
- Most groundwater occupies tiny pores between grains, fractures, and cracks — mainly in **sedimentary rocks**.

## Section 3: The Water Table and Subsurface Zones

From the surface down:
- **Zone of soil moisture** — water held by molecular attraction on soil particles; used by plants or evaporated.
- **Unsaturated (vadose) zone** — pores contain both air and water; includes the soil-moisture zone and capillary fringe.
- **Capillary fringe** — water drawn up above the water table by surface tension.
- **Water table** — the upper limit of the zone of saturation. Its shape is usually a subdued replica of the surface topography; varies seasonally and with pumping; cannot be observed directly except where it meets the surface.
- **Zone of saturation (phreatic zone)** — all pore spaces completely filled with water; water here is **groundwater**.

### Groundwater and Streams
Water flows down the slope of the water table (the **hydraulic gradient**) from **recharge areas** (topographic highs) to **discharge areas** (topographic lows: streams, lakes, springs).
- **Gaining stream** — water table is higher than the stream; groundwater flows in through the streambed.
- **Losing stream** — water table is lower than the stream; stream water seeps out into the ground.
- A single stream can gain in some reaches and lose in others.

## Section 4: Aquifers, Aquitards, and Groundwater Flow

- **Aquifer** — permeable rock/sediment that transmits groundwater freely (sands, gravels, sandstone, fractured rock). ~**80% of U.S. aquifers** are unconsolidated sand and gravel.
- **Aquitard** — impermeable layer that hinders water movement (clay, shale, unfractured igneous/metamorphic rock).
- **Unconfined aquifer** reaches the ground surface; **confined aquifer** lies beneath an aquitard.
- **Darcy's law** measures the volume of water flowing through an aquifer using the hydraulic gradient (water-table slope), hydraulic conductivity (combines permeability and fluid viscosity), and cross-sectional area.
- Groundwater moves **slowly** — ~1 foot/day in permeable deposits, roughly **10,000× slower than surface water**. Deep regional flow can take centuries to millennia.

## Section 5: Wells and Artesian Systems

- **Well** — a hole bored into the zone of saturation. Pumping causes **drawdown** and a **cone of depression** (cone-shaped lowering of the water table) around the well; this locally steepens the hydraulic gradient. Around clusters of wells, cones of depression can be miles wide.
- **Perched water table** — forms where an aquitard sits above the main water table, holding a local saturated zone.
- **Artesian system** — groundwater under pressure rises above the level of the aquifer. Requires: (1) water confined to an inclined aquifer, and (2) aquitards above and below (a confined aquifer). The **potentiometric surface** is the pressure level to which water would rise.
  - **Nonflowing artesian well** — pressure surface is below ground.
  - **Flowing artesian well** — pressure surface is above ground (water flows out on its own).
  - Municipal water towers create artificial artesian systems (tower = recharge area, pipes = confined aquifer, faucets = flowing wells).

## Section 6: Springs, Hot Springs, and Geysers

- **Spring** — groundwater outflow at the surface where fractures, faults, caves, an impermeable layer, or the water table intersects the surface.
- **Hot spring** — water 6–9 °C warmer than the local mean annual air temperature; **>1,000 in the U.S.** Over 95% are heated by cooling igneous rock; some by the geothermal gradient (e.g., Warm Springs, GA).
- **Geyser** — an intermittent hot spring that erupts columns of water. Groundwater in underground chambers within hot igneous rock is heated under pressure until superheated water flashes to steam and erupts (e.g., Old Faithful, Yellowstone).
- **Geyser/spring deposits** — chemical sedimentary rock precipitated from dissolved minerals: **siliceous sinter (geyserite)** from dissolved silica, **travertine (calcareous tufa)** from dissolved calcium carbonate (e.g., Mammoth Hot Springs).

## Section 7: Groundwater as a Resource — The Ogallala Aquifer

- The **Ogallala (High Plains) Aquifer** spans ~174,000 mi² from South Dakota to Texas — an unconfined sand/gravel aquifer up to 1,000 ft thick holding ~3.3 billion acre-feet (1 acre-foot = 326,000 gallons), more than the volume of Lake Huron.
- Most of the water is **"fossil water"** that entered 10,000–15,000 years ago during a wetter glacial climate; the present dry climate provides little recharge.
- Irrigation turned the former "Great American Desert" into the "Grain Belt of the U.S." Since the 1930s ~11% of the water has been used; the water table dropped 50–150 ft (up to ~60 m) in places — **mining water** (not sustainable). The **Keystone XL pipeline** raised contamination concerns where it would cross the aquifer and Nebraska Sand Hills.
- Worldwide, **agriculture is the biggest groundwater user**; ~69% of U.S. groundwater use is agricultural. By 2050, 5–7 billion people may face water stress.

## Section 8: Caverns and Karst Topography

- Most groundwater is mildly acidic (**carbonic acid** from rainwater + CO₂). Carbonic acid reacts with calcite in **limestone** to form soluble calcium bicarbonate, carried away as dissolved load.
- **Caverns** form where acidic groundwater dissolves soluble rock, most actively at or just below the water table (~17,000 U.S. caves; e.g., Mammoth Cave KY, Carlsbad Caverns NM).
- **Speleothems** — dripstone features formed as dripping water deposits **travertine**: **stalactites** (ceiling — beginning as thin, hollow **soda straws**), **stalagmites** (floor), **columns** (joined), plus **flowstone**, draperies, and curtains.
- **Karst topography** — landscape shaped by dissolving groundwater in limestone regions: irregular terrain, **sinkholes**, poor surface drainage, natural bridges, **cenotes**, and **tower karst** (residual towers where thick limestone dissolves along fractures; e.g., China).

## Section 9: Challenges of Groundwater Use

- **Groundwater overdraft** — withdrawing water faster than recharge can replenish it; not sustainable. **Center-pivot irrigation** in the High Plains is a major driver.
- **Subsidence** — settling of the ground surface. Water in pores holds grains apart; removing it lets pores collapse, compacting sediment and dropping the surface (e.g., San Joaquin Valley, CA — sank tens of feet; Leaning Tower of Pisa). Collapse over voids/caves/mines forms **sinkholes** (e.g., Winter Park, FL).
- **Saltwater intrusion** — fresh water floats as a lens above denser salt water; over-pumping in coastal areas draws salt water up into wells. Correctable with **recharge wells/basins**.

## Section 10: Groundwater Quality and Contamination

- Natural groundwater contains dissolved minerals; "hard" water (dissolved Ca/Mg) is generally still safe. Most contamination is from **human activity**.
- **Point source** (discrete, e.g., a factory outfall pipe) vs. **non-point source** (diffuse, e.g., agricultural runoff, road salt, regional acid mine drainage).
- Compared with surface water, groundwater is harder to clean: lower flow (less dilution), no sunlight/oxygen to degrade pollutants, and slow movement. Sewage can purify after passing through tens of meters of sand, but **coarse gravel and limestone (secondary porosity) aquifers are especially vulnerable**.
- Sources of pollution: landfill leachate, leaking tanks/pipes, septic seepage, acid mine drainage, agriculture, urban/industrial runoff, saltwater intrusion.
- **Woburn, Massachusetts** (the *A Civil Action* case) — TCE/PCE from five companies (W.R. Grace, Beatrice tannery, Unifirst, New England Plastics, Olympia) contaminated municipal wells G and H; 28 childhood leukemia cases and 16 deaths (1964–1986); now a **Superfund site** cleaned by pump-and-treat, UV, hydrogen peroxide, air stripping, and soil vapor extraction.
- **Cleanup steps**: understand the geology → understand the hydrology → identify contaminants and sources → choose treatment. **Treatment methods**: pump-and-treat, vapor extraction, bioremediation (nutrients/oxygen for aerobic bacteria, used for hydrocarbons), and permeable treatment beds (e.g., artificial wetlands).
