Module 9 covers the causes, measurement, distribution, hazards, and mitigation strategies for earthquakes. Topics include elastic rebound theory, seismic wave types, seismographs, magnitude scales, the relationship of earthquakes to plate boundaries, and major hazards (ground shaking, liquefaction, landslides, fires, tsunamis). The module concludes with earthquake prediction, the Cascadia Subduction Zone threat, and damage-reduction strategies.

## Section 1: What Is an Earthquake?

An **earthquake** is vibration produced by the rapid release of energy stored in rocks, most commonly caused by:
- Movement along a fault (most common)
- Inflation of a magma chamber
- Volcanic eruption
- Meteorite impact
- Human activity (e.g., fracking, reservoir filling)

### Key Vocabulary
| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Fault** | Fracture in Earth along which movement has occurred |
| **Focus (hypocenter)** | Point inside Earth where the earthquake originates |
| **Epicenter** | Point on Earth's surface directly above the focus |
| **Fault scarp** | Cliff or face formed by displacement along a fault |
| **Fault plane** | The planar surface of the fault |
| **Fault trace / fault line** | Where the fault plane intersects the land surface; rocks are shattered → often low areas where lakes form |
| **Foreshocks** | Smaller shaking events that precede the main quake |
| **Aftershocks** | Shaking that follows the main quake |

## Section 2: Elastic Rebound Theory

Developed after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

1. Rocks near plate boundaries experience **stress** (compressive, tensile, or shear) and deform elastically, storing strain energy.
2. As stress builds over time, the stored **elastic strain energy** increases.
3. When stress exceeds the strength of the rock, rupture occurs along a fault — the stored energy is **released rapidly** as seismic waves.
4. Rock that is not fractured snaps back toward its original shape (elastic rebound).

- **Stress**: force per unit area on rocks.
- **Strain**: deformation induced by stress.

## Section 3: Seismic Waves

Vibrations that travel outward from the focus in all directions.

### Body Waves (travel through Earth's interior)

| Wave | Speed | Travel Medium | Motion |
|------|-------|--------------|--------|
| **P-waves** (Primary / Compressional) | Fastest | Solids, liquids, gases | Parallel to propagation direction (push-pull) |
| **S-waves** (Secondary / Shear) | Slower than P | Solids only | Perpendicular to propagation (up-down/side-side) |

### Surface Waves
- **Slowest** of all seismic waves.
- Cause both horizontal and rolling vertical motion.
- **Cause the most damage** to buildings.

## Section 4: Seismographs and Locating Earthquakes

- **Seismograph**: instrument that records ground motion. Frame is bolted to the ground; a suspended mass (pen on spring) records on a rotating drum.
- **Seismogram**: the written record of an earthquake.
- The first earthquake-detecting device was built in China in **132 BCE**.

### Locating the Epicenter (Triangulation)
1. Measure the **P–S wave time interval** on a seismogram (P arrives first; S arrives later).
2. Use a **travel-time graph** to convert the interval to distance from the station.
3. Draw a circle of that radius around the station on a map.
4. Repeat with **at least 3 stations** — the epicenter is where all three circles intersect.

## Section 5: Earthquake Magnitude and Intensity

### Intensity — Modified Mercalli Scale
- Based on observed property damage and human perception.
- Ranges from I (barely felt) to XII (total destruction).
- Useful for historical earthquakes and communicating damage to the public.

### Richter Magnitude
- Introduced by Charles Richter in **1935**.
- Measures the **amplitude of the largest seismic wave** on a seismogram, corrected for distance.
- **Logarithmic scale**: each unit = 10× difference in wave amplitude, ~32× difference in energy released.

| Magnitude Difference | Ground Motion | Energy Released |
|----------------------|--------------|----------------|
| 1.0 | 10× | ~32× |
| 2.0 | 100× | ~1,000× |
| 3.0 | 1,000× | ~32,000× |
| 4.0 | 10,000× | ~1,000,000× |

### Moment Magnitude (Mw) — Now Standard
- Newer, more accurate scale.
- Calculated from: average slip on the fault × area of fault that slipped × strength of rock.
- Can also be modeled from seismogram data.
- Better than Richter for very large earthquakes.

## Section 6: Earthquakes and Plate Tectonics

Most earthquakes occur at or near plate boundaries. Depth varies:
- Shallowest: mid-ocean ridges and transform faults (~<20 km)
- Deepest: subduction zones (up to 700 km)

### Convergent (Subduction) Boundaries
- **Compression** → reverse and thrust faults.
- Deep-focus earthquakes (down to 700 km) define the angle of the descending slab.
- Highest-magnitude earthquakes on Earth occur here.
- Example: Tohoku, Japan 2011 (9.0 Mw); Banda Aceh, Sumatra 2004 (9.1 Mw).

### Divergent Boundaries
- **Tension** → normal faults.
- Shallow foci (<10 km); low magnitudes.

### Transform Boundaries
- **Shear** → strike-slip faults.
- Shallow foci (~20 km); moderate-to-large magnitudes.
- Most occur at mid-ocean ridges, but continental transforms (San Andreas Fault) can be deadly.
- San Francisco 1906: ~7.9 Mw; fault ruptured >280 miles with up to 15 ft displacement.

### Intraplate Earthquakes
Earthquakes that occur away from plate boundaries:
- **Reactivated pre-existing faults** — New Madrid, Missouri 1811–1812 (three quakes Mw 7.5–7.7); felt over 3 million mi²; rang church bells in Boston; Mississippi River temporarily reversed flow.
- **Hot spots** — magma movement.
- **Human activity** — injection wells (fracking), reservoir impoundment.

## Section 7: Earthquake Hazards

Amount of destruction depends on:
1. **Magnitude and duration** — larger quakes shake longer; longer shaking = more damage.
2. **Distance from epicenter/focus** — damage decreases with distance.
3. **Geology** — unconsolidated sediment and landfill amplify shaking 4–5× vs. solid bedrock (Mexico City 1985 example).
4. **Slope stability** — steep slopes are prone to landslides.
5. **Building style** — rigid materials (brick, unreinforced concrete, mud) fail; open first-floor parking is a vulnerability.
6. **Time of day** — determines occupancy of buildings.
7. **Population density and wealth** — poor countries have worse building codes and fewer emergency resources.

### Resonance
Buildings sway at ~0.1 seconds per story. If seismic wave frequency matches a building's natural period, **resonance** amplifies shaking catastrophically (Mexico City 1985: mid-rise buildings collapsed while shorter/taller survived).

### Liquefaction
- Loosely packed, **water-saturated sediment** loses strength during shaking and behaves like a fluid.
- Buildings sink or tilt; buried structures can float upward.
- **Sand blows / sand volcanoes** eject liquefied material to the surface.
- Example: 1964 Good Friday Earthquake, Alaska (9.2 Mw); 1989 Loma Prieta, California.

### Landslides
Earthquakes can trigger debris flows and rock avalanches, especially in mountainous terrain.
- Example: 1959 Hebgen Lake, Montana (7.3 Mw) — Madison River landslide killed 28, dammed river to form Earthquake Lake.

### Fires
Ruptured gas lines ignite fires; water-line damage hampers firefighting.
- Example: 1906 San Francisco — many deaths from fire, not shaking.

## Section 8: Tsunamis

**Tsunami** (Japanese: "harbor wave") — also called a seismic sea wave; NOT a tidal wave.

### Causes
- Underwater earthquakes with **vertical seafloor displacement** (most common).
- Submarine or coastal **landslides** entering the ocean.
- **Volcanic caldera explosions** on islands (Krakatau 1883).

### Tsunami vs. Normal Ocean Waves

| Property | Ocean Wave | Tsunami |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Speed | 8–100 kph | 800–1,000 kph |
| Wave period | 5–20 sec | 10 min – 2 hr |
| Wavelength | 100–200 m | 100–500 km |
| Motion | Circular | Straight (like a wall of water) |

- In deep ocean, tsunamis are barely noticeable but travel fast.
- As they enter shallow water, they slow and **height increases dramatically**.
- Warning sign: ocean rapidly **drains away** from shore before the wave arrives → **run immediately**.

### Major Case Studies
**2004 Banda Aceh, Sumatra**
- 9.1 Mw; third-largest earthquake in recorded history.
- Fault ruptured 1,200 km with up to 20 m vertical displacement; lasted ~7 minutes.
- 227,898 killed in 13 countries; waves up to 30 m (98 ft) tall.

**2011 Tohoku, Japan**
- 9.0 Mw; fourth-largest recorded earthquake.
- Tsunami up to 29.4 m (97 ft); 20,896 killed; $309 billion in damages.
- Meltdowns at three reactors at Fukushima Daiichi; Cs-137 release ~40% of Chernobyl's.
- Fukushima cleanup expected to take 30–40 years at cost of $187+ billion.

## Section 9: Seismic Risk Zones

### San Andreas Fault / "The Big One"
- 62% probability of a >6.7 Mw quake in San Francisco Bay Area by 2032.
- **Seismic gap** — a section of fault that has not ruptured recently is considered overdue.
- 1906 rupture: 7.9 Mw; 3,000+ killed mostly from fire.

### Cascadia Subduction Zone
- Extends 800 miles offshore, Pacific Northwest.
- Geologic evidence of 41 events in last 10,000 years; 19 ruptured the full length.
- Last major event: **January 26, 1700** (9.0 Mw) — confirmed by Japanese tsunami records.
- 10% probability of a >9.0 Mw event in the next 50 years.
- Tsunami models predict 70–90 ft (20–30 m) waves — similar to Banda Aceh.
- Five at-risk cities: Vancouver, Victoria, Seattle, Portland, Sacramento (~11 million people).
- 80–90% chance it would also trigger a major San Andreas rupture.

## Section 10: Predicting and Mitigating Earthquakes

### Prediction
Currently **not possible** to reliably predict earthquakes (time, location, magnitude).

### Mitigation Strategies
1. **Land use planning** — avoid building on active fault traces, weak soils, or unstable slopes.
2. **Building codes** — use reinforced concrete, wood-frame construction, base isolation (shock-absorbing foundations).
3. **Resonance awareness** — match building height to local soil amplification periods.
4. **Infrastructure engineering** — retrofit bridges, overpasses, and pipelines.
5. **Emergency preparedness** — early warning systems, tsunami warning buoys (NOAA), evacuation routes.
6. **Education** — know to run from the ocean when ground shakes or water recedes.
