A **stream** is any body of water flowing in a channel; a **river** is simply a large stream fed by smaller tributaries. Running water is the single most important agent shaping Earth's land surface, collecting precipitation and groundwater across drainage basins and carrying it — along with sediment — toward the sea. This page covers how streams organize, flow, and are measured; their erosional and depositional work is on [[Stream Erosion and Deposition]].

## Drainage Basins and Networks

- **Drainage basin (watershed)** — the entire region drained by a single river system.
- **Drainage divide** — the high ground separating adjacent basins.
- **Drainage network** — the interconnected set of streams that collects water over a large area.
- **Slope (gradient)** — the channel's vertical drop over horizontal distance.
- **Base level** — the lowest level to which a stream can erode (ultimate base level is sea level; local base levels include lakes, resistant rock, and larger rivers).

Streams are supplied by **snowmelt and rainfall** in the headwaters and by **groundwater** inflow, which sustains flow between storms. **Ephemeral streams** flow only after rain because they sit above the water table. Whether precipitation reaches a stream depends on the runoff-vs-infiltration balance (see [[Groundwater]] and [[Weathering and Soil]]).

## Drainage Patterns

The geometry of a drainage network reflects the underlying rock:

| Pattern | Develops on |
|---------|-------------|
| **Dendritic** (treelike) | Uniform material — the most common pattern |
| **Radial** | Volcanic cones or domes ([[Volcanoes]]) |
| **Rectangular** | Jointed or faulted bedrock |
| **Trellis** | Alternating resistant and weak strata (folded/tilted beds) |

## The Three Zones of a River System

1. **Sediment production** — erosion-dominated headwaters where sediment is generated by bedrock breakdown, bank erosion, and channel scour.
2. **Sediment transport** — trunk streams move material downstream; in balance, erosion equals deposition.
3. **Sediment deposition** — at the mouth, energy drops and sediment is laid down; only fine sediment typically reaches the ocean.

## Streamflow and Velocity

- Flow is **laminar** (smooth and straight) or **turbulent** (erratic, with vertical and horizontal mixing); natural streams are usually **turbulent**.
- Velocity increases with:
  - **Gradient** — steeper slopes give more gravitational energy.
  - **Channel shape** — a small **wetted perimeter** (contact area between water and channel) relative to cross-sectional area means less friction; **narrow, deep channels** flow most efficiently.
  - **Channel size and smoothness** — larger, smoother channels flow faster; boulders and woody debris add roughness and turbulence that slow flow.

## Discharge

**Discharge (Q)** is the volume of water passing a point per unit time:

> **Q = A × v**  (A = cross-sectional area, v = velocity), measured in m³/s or ft³/s.

Discharge is controlled by channel width, channel depth, and flow velocity. *(Example: 200 ft² × 6 ft/s = 1,200 ft³/s; widening to 800 ft² at the same velocity gives 4,800 ft³/s.)*

## Longitudinal Profile — Changes Downstream

A **longitudinal profile** runs from the **head** (source) to the **mouth** (where the stream empties into a larger body). It is typically **concave-up**. From head to mouth:
- **Discharge increases** (tributaries add water)
- **Channel size and velocity increase**
- **Gradient (slope) decreases**
