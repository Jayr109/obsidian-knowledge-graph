Earthquake hazards encompass the full range of destructive phenomena triggered by seismic shaking — from direct ground motion to secondary effects like liquefaction, landslides, fires, and tsunamis. The severity of damage depends on both physical factors (magnitude, geology) and human factors (building quality, population density, wealth).

## Factors Controlling Earthquake Damage

1. **Magnitude and duration** — larger quakes shake longer; longer shaking causes exponentially more damage.
2. **Distance from focus/epicenter** — damage decreases with distance.
3. **Local geology** — unconsolidated sediments and water-saturated fill amplify shaking 4–5× vs. solid bedrock. Mexico City 1985: lake-bed clay amplified waves by this factor.
4. **Slope stability** — steep slopes fail more easily under shaking.
5. **Building construction**:
   - **Vulnerable**: brick, stone, mud, unreinforced concrete, buildings with open first-floor parking.
   - **Safer**: wood-frame single-family homes (in the US), base-isolated structures.
   - **Resonance**: if a building's natural sway period matches dominant seismic wave period, shaking is catastrophically amplified (Mexico City 1985 — mid-rise buildings collapsed while short and tall ones survived).
6. **Time of day** — occupancy of workplaces, schools, public buildings.
7. **Population density and per capita income** — poorer countries have weaker building codes, fewer first responders, and fewer resources to recover.

> "Earthquakes don't kill people, buildings do."

## Primary Hazard: Ground Shaking

The direct vibration of Earth's surface. **Surface waves** cause the most structural damage (rolling horizontal + vertical motion). Amplification occurs over soft sediment.

## Secondary Hazard: Liquefaction

**Liquefaction** occurs when loosely packed, **water-saturated sediments** lose strength during intense shaking and behave as a fluid.

- Buildings can sink, tilt, or collapse.
- Buried tanks and pipes can float upward.
- **Sand blows** (sand volcanoes) appear where pressurized liquefied material erupts to the surface.
- Classic examples: 1964 Good Friday Earthquake, Alaska (9.2 Mw); 1989 Loma Prieta, California; 2011 Christchurch, New Zealand.

**Mitigation**: avoid building on water-saturated loose sediment; use deep pilings to anchor foundations in bedrock.

## Secondary Hazard: Landslides

Seismic shaking can trigger rock falls, debris flows, and large-scale slope failures.
- Nepal 2015 (7.8 Mw): landslides in remote mountain villages cut off rescue and supply routes.
- 1959 Hebgen Lake, Montana (7.3 Mw): landslide buried a campground (28 killed) and dammed the Madison River, creating Earthquake Lake.
- Mt. Everest 1915: avalanche triggered by Nepal quake killed 24 at Base Camp.

## Secondary Hazard: Fires

Earthquakes rupture gas lines and electrical systems. Water-line breaks hamper firefighting.
- 1906 San Francisco: most deaths and property destruction were from fire, not shaking.
- 2011 Tohoku: massive fires at oil and gas facilities.

## Secondary Hazard: Tsunamis

Tsunamis are covered extensively on this page and in [[Earthquakes]].

**Key facts:**
- Caused by vertical displacement of the seafloor (most common), submarine landslides, or volcanic caldera collapse.
- Travel at 800–1,000 kph in deep ocean; slow as they approach shore and height increases dramatically.
- Wavelength 100–500 km vs. 100–200 m for normal waves — tsunamis carry a massive volume of water inland.
- Warning sign: ocean **drains away** from shore → run to high ground immediately.

### 2004 Banda Aceh, Sumatra (9.1 Mw)
- 3rd-largest earthquake in recorded history.
- Fault ruptured 1,200 km; vertical displacement up to 20 m; lasted ~7 minutes.
- 227,898 killed in 13 countries; waves up to 30 m (98 ft).

### 2011 Tohoku, Japan (9.0 Mw)
- 4th-largest recorded; tsunami up to 29.4 m (97 ft).
- 20,896 killed; $309 billion in damages.
- Triggered partial meltdowns at Fukushima Daiichi (3 reactors); Cs-137 released ~40% of Chernobyl amount.
- Cleanup expected to take 30–40 years, cost $187+ billion.

## Damage Comparison: Haiti vs. Chile (2010)

| | Haiti | Chile |
|-|-------|-------|
| Magnitude | 7.0 Mw | 8.8 Mw |
| Deaths | 316,000+ | ~500 |
| Per capita GDP | Low | Much higher |
| Building codes | Poorly enforced | Strict, enforced |

Haiti's tragedy illustrates how poverty amplifies earthquake lethality far beyond what magnitude alone predicts.

## Mitigation Strategies

| Strategy | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Land use planning** | Avoid active fault traces, weak soils, steep slopes, floodable low-lying coastal areas |
| **Building codes** | Require reinforced structures; mandate base isolation (shock-absorbing foundations) for critical buildings |
| **Resonance design** | Match building period to local amplification data; avoid soil/building period matching |
| **Infrastructure retrofit** | Seismically upgrade bridges, overpasses, pipelines, dams |
| **Early warning systems** | USGS ShakeAlert; seconds of warning can allow bracing or automatic shutoffs |
| **Tsunami warning network** | NOAA deep-ocean buoys (DART buoys) detect tsunami waves; broadcast warnings to coastal communities |
| **Public education** | Earthquake drills; "Drop, Cover, Hold On"; coastal tsunami evacuation routes; recognizing ocean retreat as warning sign |
