**Karst topography** is a landscape shaped mainly by the dissolving power of [[Groundwater]], developing in moist regions underlain by soluble bedrock — most commonly **limestone**. The same process that carves underground caverns produces a distinctive surface of sinkholes, disappearing streams, and residual towers. Karst represents groundwater acting as an **erosional agent**.

## The Dissolution Process

Most groundwater is mildly acidic. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and decaying plants to form weak **carbonic acid (H₂CO₃)**. This acid reacts with **calcite** in limestone to produce soluble **calcium bicarbonate**, which is carried away in solution as dissolved load. This is the same chemical weathering reaction (carbonation) described in [[Weathering and Soil]].

## Caverns

- **Caverns** are the most spectacular result of groundwater erosion — about **17,000 caves** have been found in the U.S. (famously Mammoth Cave, Kentucky and Carlsbad Caverns, New Mexico).
- They form as acidic groundwater dissolves limestone bedrock, with the most active erosion **at or just below the water table**.
- Caverns commonly develop at several levels as the water table drops over time.

## Speleothems (Dripstone Features)

Once a cave is air-filled, dripping and flowing water that evaporates deposits **travertine** (a form of calcium carbonate), building **speleothems**:

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Soda straw** | A thin, hollow tube of calcite hanging from the ceiling — the earliest growth stage of a stalactite. Water drips through its hollow center; when the tube clogs, water flows down the outside and it thickens into a stalactite. |
| **Stalactite** | Hangs from the cave ceiling (grows downward) |
| **Stalagmite** | Builds up from the cave floor |
| **Column** | Forms when a stalactite and stalagmite join |
| **Flowstone** | Layered travertine from thin films of water on walls/floors |
| **Draperies / curtains** | Wavy sheets that thicken as deposits build |

## Karst Surface Features

- **Sinkholes (sinks)** — closed depressions formed as groundwater dissolves bedrock, often with sudden collapse over a void; common over limestone, salt layers/domes, and abandoned mines (e.g., Winter Park, FL).
- **Cenotes** — water-filled sinkholes exposing the water table.
- **Disappearing streams** — a striking lack of surface drainage, because water drains into the subsurface.
- **Natural bridges** — remnants of collapsed cave roofs.
- **Tower karst** — steep residual towers left where thick, highly fractured limestone dissolves along joints (classic examples in China).
