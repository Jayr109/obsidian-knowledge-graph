A **flood** is an overbank flow condition in which a stream's discharge exceeds its channel capacity. Floods are the **most common and most costly natural disaster** in the world — U.S. flooding averages over **$2 billion** in damage and ~**100 deaths** per year. This page covers flood measurement, types, causes, and control; the underlying streamflow physics is on [[Streams and Rivers]].

## Key Terms

- **Stage** — the height of the water surface at a location and time.
- **Lag time** — the delay between peak rainfall and the onset/peak of flooding.
- **Hydrograph** — a graph of a river's discharge over time.
- **Recurrence interval** — how often a flood of a given size is expected. A **"100-year flood"** has a **1% probability in any given year**; a 25-year flood is smaller but four times as likely. Reliable estimates need 20–30 years of stream-gage data.

## Flood Types

| Type | Cause |
|------|-------|
| **Regional flood** | Seasonal — rain on snowpack, frozen or already-saturated ground (e.g., Mississippi River, 1993) |
| **Flash flood** | High-intensity rain funneled into narrow channels — mountains, deserts, urban areas; **50% of deaths occur in vehicles** |
| **Storm-surge flood** | Onshore hurricane winds + low pressure push water onto land (to 20–30 ft); cause **9 of 10 hurricane fatalities** (Galveston, 1900: 8,000–12,000 dead) |

## Causes and Damage

Flooding magnitude depends on the precipitation, the basin's size/shape/slope, the soil and bedrock's storage capacity, and **human modification** of the landscape:
- Urbanization adds **impervious surfaces** (roads, roofs, lots) and storm sewers that rush water to streams.
- Wetland destruction removes natural storage ([[Wetlands]]).
- Channelization and farm tile drains speed runoff.

Damage includes loss of life, destruction of structures and crops, livestock loss, **contamination** (sewage, agricultural chemicals, petroleum — see [[Water Pollution and Contamination]]), and sediment burial of floodplains.

## Case Studies

- **Big Thompson Canyon, CO (July 31, 1976)** — a stationary storm dumped >12 in of rain (8 in in one hour); a 2-ft trickle became a 19-ft torrent. **144 deaths**, $39M damage — the worst flash flood in U.S. history.
- **Death Valley, CA (2015)** — a "1,000-year storm" caused flash floods; $47M damage to Scotty's Castle.
- **Zion / Keyhole Canyon, UT (2015)** — flash floods killed 20, including 7 experienced canyoneers.

## Flood Control

**Structural** measures:
- **Dams and reservoirs** — store and release water (the Colorado River is the most controlled river in the U.S.; Hoover Dam → Lake Mead, Glen Canyon Dam → Lake Powell).
- **Channelization (floodways)** — straightened/lined channels move water fast but destroy stream ecosystems (e.g., Los Angeles River).
- **Levees** — raised embankments that contain floodwaters; water-saturated levees can fail even without being overtopped.
- **Floodwalls** — concrete walls, sometimes with drop-in gates (e.g., Cape Girardeau, MO).

**Nonstructural** measures: relocating settlements (Valmeyer, IL moved off the floodplain after 1993), floodplain land-use limits, **wetland restoration**, building codes, and the **National Flood Insurance** program (available only from the federal government; often required for floodplain properties).

### Dam Failures
Dams "all can and do fail," sometimes catastrophically:
- **Johnstown, PA (May 31, 1889)** — South Fork Dam failed; **2,209 killed in minutes**; a 35–40 ft wave hit the town.
- **Teton Dam, ID (1976)** — failed on filling; 11 deaths, 13,000 cattle lost.
- **St. Francis Dam, CA (1928)** — a famous engineering-failure disaster.
