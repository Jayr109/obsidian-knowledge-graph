**Groundwater** is water stored beneath Earth's surface in the pore spaces, fractures, and cracks of rock and sediment. Its ultimate source is **precipitation**, and although only a small fraction of Earth's water, groundwater makes up **over 96% of the planet's unfrozen freshwater** and supplies about a quarter of U.S. freshwater use. It is stored according to **porosity** and moves according to **permeability**, flowing slowly from recharge areas to discharge areas under gravity.

## Recharge: Runoff vs. Infiltration

When precipitation reaches the ground it either runs off the surface or soaks in. The split is a "battle" between:
- **Overland flow (runoff)** — occurs when the precipitation rate exceeds the infiltration rate.
- **Infiltration** — water soaking into the subsurface to become groundwater.

Infiltration is favored by gentle slopes, porous/permeable soils, low-intensity rain or snowmelt, and vegetation. Runoff is increased by steep slopes, impermeable ground, high-intensity rain, and land-use changes (urbanization, clear-cutting, poor agriculture). See [[Weathering and Soil]] for soil controls on infiltration.

## Porosity and Permeability

| Property | Definition | Controls | Determines |
|----------|-----------|----------|-----------|
| **Porosity** | % of empty (pore) space | Grain size/shape, sorting, packing, fractures | How much water is stored |
| **Permeability** | Ability to transmit fluid | Connectivity between pores | How fast water moves |

Well-sorted sediments have high porosity; poorly sorted sediments have low porosity. Most crystalline rocks gain porosity only through fractures. Most groundwater is held in **sedimentary rocks**.

## The Water Table and Subsurface Zones

From the surface downward:
- **Unsaturated (vadose) zone** — pores hold both air and water; includes the **zone of soil moisture** (used by plants/evaporation) and the **capillary fringe** (water held above the water table by surface tension).
- **Water table** — the upper surface of the zone of saturation. It mimics surface topography in a subdued way and rises/falls with the seasons and with pumping.
- **Zone of saturation (phreatic zone)** — all pores filled with water; this water is groundwater.

## Aquifers and Aquitards

- **Aquifer** — permeable rock/sediment that transmits water freely (sand, gravel, sandstone, fractured rock). About **80% of U.S. aquifers** are unconsolidated sand and gravel.
- **Aquitard** — low-permeability layer that impedes flow (clay, shale, unfractured igneous/metamorphic rock).
- **Unconfined aquifer** — reaches the surface and is recharged directly; vulnerable to surface contamination.
- **Confined aquifer** — sandwiched between aquitards; recharged only through its exposed recharge zone.

## Movement of Groundwater

Groundwater flows from **recharge areas** (topographic highs) to **discharge areas** (topographic lows — streams, lakes, springs) down the slope of the water table.
- **Hydraulic gradient** — the slope of the water table; sets the direction and velocity of flow.
- **Darcy's law** — volume of flow depends on the hydraulic gradient, **hydraulic conductivity** (permeability + fluid viscosity), and cross-sectional area.
- Flow is **slow**: ~1 ft/day in permeable deposits, roughly **10,000× slower than surface water**; deep regional flow takes centuries to millennia.

Streams interacting with the water table are **gaining** (water table above the stream, groundwater seeps in) or **losing** (water table below the stream, stream water seeps out).

## Wells and Artesian Systems

- A **well** taps the zone of saturation. Pumping causes **drawdown** and a **cone of depression** in the water table; clusters of wells create cones miles across.
- A **perched water table** forms where a local aquitard holds water above the main water table.
- An **artesian system** confines water in an inclined aquifer between two aquitards so that pressure drives water above the aquifer level. A **flowing artesian well** reaches the surface on its own; a **nonflowing** one does not. Municipal water towers are artificial artesian systems.

## Springs, Hot Springs, and Geysers

- **Springs** discharge groundwater where the water table, a fracture, fault, cave, or impermeable layer meets the surface.
- **Hot springs** are 6–9 °C above local mean air temperature; >95% are heated by cooling **igneous rock** (see [[Hot Spots and Mantle Plumes]]).
- **Geysers** (e.g., Old Faithful) erupt when groundwater in chambers above hot igneous rock superheats and flashes to steam. Their mineral deposits — **siliceous sinter/geyserite** (silica) and **travertine** (calcium carbonate) — are chemical [[Sedimentary Rocks]].
