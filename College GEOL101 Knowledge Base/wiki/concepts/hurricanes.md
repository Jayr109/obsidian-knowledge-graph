**Hurricanes** are the greatest storms on Earth and the ultimate coastal hazard — intense tropical low-pressure systems that combine destructive winds, torrential rain, and a deadly **storm surge**. The same storm is called a **hurricane** in the western Atlantic and eastern Pacific, a **typhoon** in the western North Pacific, and a **cyclone** in the Indian Ocean. Because most of the U.S. population lives within 75 km of a coast, hurricanes put millions at risk. They are part of the broader picture of [[Coastal Hazards and Protection]].

## Formation

Hurricanes need specific conditions and form in **late summer and early fall**:
- **Warm ocean water** — sea-surface temperatures must reach about **27 °C** to supply heat and moisture.
- **Latitude between ~5° and 20°** — far enough from the equator for the **Coriolis effect** to spin the storm (it is too weak within 5° of the equator), but where water is warm.

A disturbance intensifies through stages: **tropical depression → tropical storm** (sustained winds ≥ 39 mph, gets a name) **→ hurricane** (sustained winds ≥ 74 mph).

## Structure

A hurricane is an intense low-pressure center; the **pressure gradient** (how fast pressure changes with distance) drives the winds — a steeper gradient means stronger winds.
- The **eye** is the calm, low-pressure center, where winds and rain die down.
- The **eye wall** — a ring of towering cumulonimbus clouds around the eye — has the **greatest wind speeds and heaviest rain**.
- Storms rotate **counterclockwise** in the Northern Hemisphere, with airflow spiraling in at the surface and out near the top.

## Measuring Intensity

The **Saffir-Simpson scale** ranks hurricanes Category 1–5 by wind speed, eye pressure, and storm surge:

| Category | Winds (mph) | Surge (ft) | Damage |
|----------|-------------|------------|--------|
| 1 | 74–95 | 4–5 | Minimal |
| 2 | 96–110 | 6–8 | Moderate |
| 3 | 111–130 | 9–12 | Extensive |
| 4 | 131–155 | 13–18 | Extreme |
| 5 | >155 | >18 | Catastrophic |

A **major hurricane** is Category 3 or greater.

## Destruction

- **Storm surge** — the most devastating effect: a dome of water 65–80 km wide that sweeps ashore where the eye lands. In the Northern Hemisphere it is worst on the **right side of the eye**, where winds blow toward shore.
- **Wind damage** — affects a wider area than surge; hurricanes can spawn tornadoes.
- **Heavy rain and inland flooding** — can strike hundreds of km inland, days after landfall.

## Case Studies

- **Galveston, Texas (1900)** — built on a low barrier island with no adequate warning; 8,000–12,000 died, making it the **deadliest natural disaster in U.S. history**. Survivors built a 17-ft seawall and raised the city.
- **Hurricane Katrina (2005)** — a Category 3 at landfall whose surge caused **levee and floodwall failures** that flooded **80% of New Orleans**; >1,833 deaths and ~$108 billion in damage.
- **Hurricane Harvey (2017)** — a Category 4 that stalled over Texas and dropped record rainfall (>50 inches), causing catastrophic flooding in Houston.

Atlantic hurricane activity has been rising, and as sea level climbs with climate change, coastal hurricane damage is expected to increase.
