Coastlines are dynamic, but people want them to stay put — and that tension drives the hazards and engineering covered here. As global sea level rises, **coastal erosion**, **tidal flooding**, and storm impacts are all increasing. This page covers the hazards of living at the shore and the structural and non-structural ways people try to hold the line. The underlying physics of waves and currents is on [[Coastal Processes]]; the biggest storms are on [[Hurricanes]].

## Coastal Hazards

- **Storms** — hurricanes/typhoons and nor'easters (see [[Hurricanes]]).
- **Tidal flooding** — high tide combined with a storm surge, or a **king tide** (the year's highest tides, when Sun–Moon–Earth align near perihelion/perigee). Tidal flooding has risen 300–925% on U.S. coasts since the 1960s.
- **Tsunamis** — often from offshore earthquakes or [[Mass Movement|underwater landslides]].
- **Coastal erosion** — averages ~3.3 ft/yr on the Atlantic and Gulf coasts; worst on loose, unconsolidated sediment and accelerated by storm surge.

**Sea-cliff erosion** is worsened by **salt wedging** (salt-crystal growth in pores), urban **runoff**, lawn watering, and the weight of structures (homes, pools) built on the cliff edge.

## Why Beaches Erode: Sediment Starvation

Beaches survive only if sediment supply keeps up with loss. Coasts are **starved** of replenishing sand by:
- **Dams** on rivers that trap sediment upstream.
- **Coastal structures** (groins, jetties, breakwaters) that interrupt longshore transport.
- **Dredging and ship channels** that carry sand offshore.

## Hard (Structural) Protection

All hard structures interfere with the natural longshore sediment "river," so they typically shift erosion to a neighboring stretch of coast:

| Structure | Orientation | Effect |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **Seawall** | Parallel to shore | Protects the backshore but can starve and steepen the beach |
| **Groin** | Perpendicular to shore | Traps sand **updrift**, erodes **downdrift** |
| **Jetty** | Paired at river/harbor mouths | Keeps inlets open; traps sand updrift; must be dredged |
| **Breakwater** | Offshore, usually parallel | Shelters harbors; traps sand updrift, erodes downdrift |

Seawalls can fail or be overtopped (Galveston, Japan 2011 tsunami) and may give a false sense of security.

## Soft (Non-Structural) Protection and Management

- **Beach nourishment** — trucking or pumping in sand (e.g., Miami Beach). It boosts tourism and storm protection but is **temporary** and must be repeated.
- **Natural buffers** — tall dunes, wide beaches, and offshore bars absorb wave energy; dune stabilization helps protect barrier-island communities (Outer Banks, NC).
- **Land-use management** — **setback zones** (the **E-60 line** marks the shore's projected position in 60 years), zoning, insurance controls, real-estate disclosure, and **relocation**. The **Cape Hatteras Lighthouse** was moved 2,900 ft inland in 1999 to escape the advancing shore.
