A **glacier** is a thick mass of ice that forms over hundreds to thousands of years by the **accumulation, compaction, and recrystallization of snow**, and that moves under its own weight. Glaciers are part of two great Earth systems — the **hydrologic cycle** (water can be locked in ice for tens of thousands of years) and the **rock cycle** (ice erodes, transports, and deposits sediment). This page covers the types of glaciers, how glacial ice forms and moves, and the glacial budget; the landforms glaciers leave behind are on [[Glacial Landforms]].

## Types of Glaciers

- **Valley (alpine) glaciers** — flow down mountain valleys from an accumulation center.
- **Ice sheets** — continental-scale masses that flow outward in all directions from accumulation centers. Today only **Greenland** (~1.7 million km², ~1,500 m thick) and **Antarctica** (~13.9 million km², glaciated for ~30 million years) host them. If Antarctica's ice melted, sea level would rise ~70 m. (Arctic **sea ice**, up to ~4 m thick, is frozen ocean — not glacial ice, which forms on land.)
- **Ice shelves** — the floating seaward extension of a glacier or ice sheet where it flows into deep water; fed from landward, some are breaking up with climate change.
- **Ice caps, outlet glaciers, and piedmont glaciers** — smaller upland ice masses, tongues draining larger masses, and lobes that spread out where an alpine glacier emerges from its valley.

## How Glacial Ice Forms

The **recipe for glaciation** is a cold climate, more snowfall than melt, and a lack of mass-wasting losses like avalanches. Snow then transforms in stages: snow → **granular snow** → **firn** (compacted, recrystallized granules) → interlocking crystals of **glacial ice**.

## Glacial Movement

Glacial ice, though solid, flows in two ways:
- **Plastic flow** — internal movement; under pressure, ice deep in the glacier behaves as a plastic.
- **Basal slip** — the whole ice mass slides along the ground, lubricated by **meltwater**.

The brittle upper ~50 m is the **zone of fracture**, where **crevasses** open as the glacier moves over irregular or steep terrain. Like a river, a glacier does not move uniformly — flow is **fastest in the center**, slowed by drag along the valley walls and floor.

## The Glacial Budget

A glacier is divided into two zones separated by the **snowline**:
- **Zone of accumulation** — above the snowline, where snow input exceeds loss.
- **Zone of wastage** — below it, where there is a net loss of ice by **ablation** (melting and **calving** — the breaking off of icebergs where a glacier meets the sea).

The **glacial budget** is the balance between accumulation and loss:
- Accumulation > loss → the **glacial front advances**.
- Loss > accumulation → the front **retreats**.

Because glaciers respond directly to temperature and precipitation, they are sensitive **climate indicators** — and today most glaciers are retreating.
