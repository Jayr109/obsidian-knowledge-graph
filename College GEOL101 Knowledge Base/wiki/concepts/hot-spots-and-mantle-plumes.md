Hot spots are localized regions of volcanic activity that are not located at plate boundaries, but are instead thought to be caused by stationary columns of hot mantle rock rising from depth.

## Mantle Plume
- A **mantle plume** is a cylindrically shaped upwelling of abnormally hot rock rising through the mantle.
- Plumes are thought to originate from the deep mantle and remain relatively stationary for long periods of geologic time.

## Hot Spot
- The **hot spot** is the surface volcanic expression of a mantle plume.
- As a lithospheric plate moves over a stationary hot spot, successive volcanoes form → a **hot-spot track** (chain of volcanoes with systematically increasing age away from the active hot spot).

## Examples

### Hawaiian Islands (Oceanic Hot Spot)
- Oceanic crust (Pacific Plate) moves over the Hawaiian hot spot.
- Active volcano at one end (Kilauea / Big Island); progressively older, more eroded islands extending northwest.
- Islands become older the farther they are from the hot spot.
- Basaltic magma; shield volcanoes; quiet eruptions.
- Kilauea = world's most continuously active volcano (~60 years nearly continuous eruption).
- 2018 eruption: fissure eruptions sent lava through subdivisions; ~600 structures destroyed.

### Yellowstone (Continental Hot Spot)
- North American Plate moves over the Yellowstone hot spot.
- Continental crust setting → rhyolitic magma.
- Most explosive eruption type → caldera eruptions (supervolcano).
- Age of calderas increases along the hot-spot track (Snake River Plain).

## Role in Evidence for Plate Tectonics
- Hot-spot tracks provide a "fixed reference frame" to measure absolute plate motions.
- Age of each volcano in a chain indicates elapsed time since it was over the plume.

## Map of Hot Spots
- ~40–50 known hot spots worldwide.
- Most are not at plate boundaries.
- Well-known examples besides Hawaii and Yellowstone: Iceland (also on Mid-Atlantic Ridge), Galapagos, Canary Islands, Reunion (Indian Ocean).
