**Slope stability** is the balance between the forces that drive material downhill and the forces that hold it in place. When **driving forces** (the weight of the material acting through gravity) exceed the **resisting forces** (the shear strength of the material and the binding effect of vegetation), the slope fails as [[Mass Movement]]. This page covers what controls that balance, what triggers failure, and how slopes are stabilized.

## The Angle of Repose and the Role of Water

The **angle of repose** is the steepest angle at which a pile of loose, unconsolidated sediment remains stable; it depends on the grain size, shape, and water content. Dry sand fails above about **35°**.

Water has a two-sided effect, well illustrated by a sand castle:
- A **small amount** of water binds grains together by **cohesion** (surface tension), letting damp sand hold a steeper slope than dry sand.
- **Too much** water raises **pore pressure**, forcing grains apart and destroying cohesion — the castle (or hillside) collapses.

## What Controls Slope Stability

- **Slope angle** — steeper slopes fail more easily.
- **Material** — clays are especially weak; **bentonite** (clay from weathered volcanic ash) absorbs water and fails readily.
- **Water content** — see above.
- **Zones of weakness** — bedding planes, foliations, joints (including exfoliation joints in granite), fractures, and faults act as ready-made failure surfaces.
- **Vegetation** — roots bind grains and absorb water.
- **Climate and time** — control weathering and water delivery.

## Triggers of Slope Failure

A failure usually has a combination of causes, but the immediate **trigger** is often:
- **Volcanic eruptions** (e.g., lahars from Mt. St. Helens).
- **Earthquakes** — shaking and **liquefaction** (see [[Earthquake Hazards]]).
- **Heavy precipitation** — saturating the ground (1983 Thistle, Utah; 1985 Mameyes, Puerto Rico).
- **Land-use change** — road cuts that **oversteepen** a slope or cut into its **toe**, and **removal of vegetation** by fire, logging, or development (post-fire debris flows are especially dangerous).

## Recognizing and Preventing Landslides

**Warning signs**: cracks in foundations and walls, misaligned fences, tilted trees or poles, broken underground pipes, fractured or hummocky ground, and water seeping from the toe of a slope.

**Prevention and stabilization**:
- **Retaining walls** and **gabions** (rock-filled wire baskets).
- **Replanting vegetation** and **fencing/screening** slopes.
- **Improving drainage** to lower pore pressure.
- **Regrading** to gentler slopes.
- **Rock bolts**, slope covers, **debris-flow barriers**, and diversion channels.
