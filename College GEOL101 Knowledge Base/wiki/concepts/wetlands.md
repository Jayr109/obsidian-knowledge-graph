**Wetlands** are transitional environments where the land is saturated or covered by shallow water long enough to support water-adapted life. Often called **"nature's filters,"** they purify water, buffer floods and storm surge, and host extraordinary biodiversity. Their large-scale destruction is a central theme in the surface-water issues of [[Module 11: Rivers and Streams]].

## Defining Criteria

A wetland must meet all three conditions:
1. **Hydrology** — water present at the surface, or soils in the root zone saturated, during the growing season or longer.
2. **Hydrophytic vegetation** — water-tolerant plants (cattails, wild rice, willows, sawgrass).
3. **Hydric soils** — poorly drained soils that become **anaerobic** during the growing season.

Two broad types exist: **coastal** (saltwater/tidal marshes — ~5%) and **freshwater** (swamps, marshes, bogs — ~95%).

## Why Wetlands Matter

- **Water quality** — filter sediments and contaminants (see [[Water Pollution and Contamination]]).
- **Flood reduction** — slow and store runoff, lowering flood peaks ([[Floods and Flood Control]]).
- **Habitat** — breeding grounds for fish/shellfish and stopovers for migratory birds.
- **Storm buffering** — coastal wetlands absorb hurricane storm surge.
- **Recreation** for people.

## Wetland Loss

- Outside Alaska, U.S. wetlands have declined by ~**55% since the 1600s**, mostly from **draining for agriculture** and infilling for development.
- ~10% or less of the original wetlands remain in California, Ohio, and Iowa. The largest remaining wetlands in the lower 48 are in Texas, Florida, and Minnesota.

## Case Studies

- **Ohio's Great Black Swamp** — once covered ~4,800 mi² of NW Ohio/NE Indiana and acted as the "kidneys" filtering the Maumee River into Lake Erie's western basin. It was drained for farmland; Ohio has now lost ~90% of its wetlands. Its loss is a major contributor to the **harmful algal blooms** now threatening Lake Erie water supplies.
- **The Everglades, Florida** — wetland loss (~50% near the Everglades from sugarcane agriculture and coastal city expansion) and rerouted flow drive coastal algal blooms. The **Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan (CERP)**, begun in 2000 (~$10.5 billion total), is among the largest environmental restoration efforts on Earth — building structures like the S-333N gate to restore water flow, with completion expected by 2035 at the earliest.
