Streams do geologic work by **eroding**, **transporting**, and **depositing** sediment. The balance among these — set by velocity, discharge, and load — determines a channel's shape and the landforms it builds, from V-shaped canyons to broad deltas. This page continues from [[Streams and Rivers]].

## Erosion

Streams erode by three mechanisms:
- **Abrasion** — transported sediment scrapes, bumps, and polishes the bed; swirling sediment in eddies drills **potholes** into the streambed.
- **Hydraulic action** — the sheer force of moving water.
- **Corrosion (dissolution)** — chemical dissolving of soluble bedrock such as limestone.

Erosion acts as **downcutting** (vertical, toward base level) and **lateral erosion** (sideways, concentrated on the outer bends, or **cut banks**). Its effectiveness depends on velocity, discharge, load, rock type, relief, base level, and climate.

## Sediment Transport

A stream carries its **load** three ways:

| Load type | What moves | Controls |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **Dissolved load** | Ions in solution (mostly from groundwater) | Water chemistry, *not* velocity |
| **Suspended load** | Fine sand, silt, clay (the largest part) | Velocity and settling velocity |
| **Bed load** | Sand, gravel, boulders by **saltation**, sliding, rolling | High flow only; boulders move in rare floods |

- **Capacity** — the maximum total load a stream can carry; increases with **discharge**.
- **Competence** — the maximum particle size it can move; increases with **velocity**.

## Deposition

When velocity falls below the **settling velocity**, sediment drops out. Particles of similar size settle together — **sorting** — with the largest settling first. Stream-deposited sediment is **alluvium** (the raw material of clastic [[Sedimentary Rocks]]).

## Channel Types

| Channel | Gradient / Load | Features |
|---------|-----------------|----------|
| **Meandering** | Low gradient, fine load, one main channel | Cut banks, point bars, oxbow lakes |
| **Braided** | High gradient, coarse load, variable discharge | Maze of channels and gravel bars; common below glaciers |
| **Bedrock** | Steep headwaters, cut into solid rock | Steps and pools; pattern set by geology |

In a **meander**, the outer bend (**cut bank**) erodes while the inner bend (**point bar**) receives deposits. Bends migrate and can be cut off to form an **oxbow lake**. If base level drops or land uplifts, a meander can downcut into **incised (entrenched) meanders** (e.g., Goosenecks of the San Juan River, Utah — uplift of the Colorado Plateau).

## Stream Valleys

- **Alluvial channels** have wide, flat valley floors; **bedrock channels** carve narrow **V-shaped valleys** and, in arid climates, vertical-walled **slot canyons**.
- **Valley deepening** by downcutting (steep gradient, channel far above base level) yields V-shaped valleys with **rapids and waterfalls** (e.g., Niagara Falls retreat).
- **Valley widening** occurs as a stream nears a graded condition and meanders migrate side-to-side, producing a **floodplain**.

## Depositional Landforms

- **Floodplain** — flat valley floor inundated when the stream overtops its banks.
- **Natural levees** — raised banks built when overbank water suddenly loses velocity and drops coarse sediment beside the channel; **back swamps** and **yazoo tributaries** form behind them.
- **Deltas** — build where a sediment-laden stream enters still water; coarse **foreset beds** near the mouth, fine **bottomset beds** beyond, with the channel splitting into **distributaries**. The **Mississippi River Delta** is seven coalescing deltas, presently a **bird-foot delta**.
- **Alluvial fans** — fan-shaped deposits where a high-gradient stream leaves a narrow mountain valley and abruptly loses gradient; coarse at the apex, fine at the base; common in arid climates (e.g., Death Valley).
