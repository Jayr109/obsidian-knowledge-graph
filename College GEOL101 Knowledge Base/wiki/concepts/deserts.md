A **desert** is not defined by heat but by **dryness**: a dry climate is one where yearly precipitation is less than the potential for evaporation. Dry regions cover about **30% of Earth's land surface** and come in two grades — **desert (arid)** and **steppe (semiarid)**. Despite the lack of water, it is **running water**, not wind, that does most of the erosional work in deserts. This page covers why deserts exist and how they are shaped; wind's distinct role is on [[Wind Erosion and Deposition]].

## Why Dry Lands Exist

Four main controls produce dry climates:
- **Subsiding air masses** — bands of high pressure near **20–35° latitude** (the **"horse latitudes"**) where sinking, warming air suppresses clouds and rain. This drives the great **subtropical deserts** (e.g., the Sahara) and is ultimately powered by uneven solar heating and atmospheric circulation cells.
- **Cold ocean currents** — chill the overlying air and block its rising, creating foggy **west-coast deserts** (Atacama in South America, Namib in Africa).
- **Deep continental interiors** — far from any ocean moisture (the **Gobi** of central Asia), giving **middle-latitude deserts**.
- **Rain shadows** — as winds rise over a **mountain barrier**, air cools and drops its moisture on the windward side; dry, descending air on the leeward side forms a **rain-shadow desert** (behind the Sierra Nevada and Cascades).

**Desertification** — the spread of desert-like conditions into formerly productive dry lands — is expanding worldwide.

## Weathering in Dry Climates

With little water, **chemical weathering** is subdued and **mechanical weathering** dominates (see [[Weathering and Soil]]). A characteristic feature is **desert varnish** — a dark reddish-brown to black coating of clay minerals with manganese and iron oxides that forms very slowly over thousands of years; Native American **petroglyphs** were pecked through it.

## The Role of Water

- Most desert streambeds are dry most of the time. **Ephemeral (intermittent) streams** flow only in response to rain — sometimes only hours a year. Regional names include **wash** and **arroyo** (western U.S.), **wadi** (Arabia/North Africa), and others.
- Because dry ground sheds water rather than absorbing it, **flash floods** arrive suddenly and subside quickly.
- A few **exotic** permanent streams cross deserts by originating in well-watered mountains and carrying enough water to survive evaporation (the **Colorado** and **Nile** rivers).

## Arid Landscapes

Deserts typically have **interior drainage** — streams end in interior basins instead of reaching the ocean, so erosion proceeds toward local base levels.

- **Basin and Range** (the U.S. Southwest) — uplifted mountains shed sediment into adjacent basins. Sediment-laden flash floods build **alluvial fans** at canyon mouths; coalescing fans form a **bajada**. After rains, a shallow **playa lake** forms and then evaporates, leaving a flat, dry **playa**. Continued erosion reduces mountains to isolated knobs called **inselbergs**.
- **Colorado Plateau** — a high region drained by the Colorado River, where erosion of flat-lying strata leaves, in order of decreasing size, **mesas**, **buttes**, and **pinnacles** (e.g., Monument Valley, Arches NP). They are the same flat-lying rock at progressive stages of erosion (mesa → butte → pinnacle), distinguished by proportion:
  - **Mesa** — broad, flat-topped, and **wider than it is tall**.
  - **Butte** — flat-topped but **taller than it is wide** (a worn-down mesa).
  - **Pinnacle** — a tall, slender **spire** (a worn-down butte).
