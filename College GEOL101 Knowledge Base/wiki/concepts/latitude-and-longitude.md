**Latitude and longitude** form the spherical coordinate system used to specify any location on Earth. It is one of the oldest methods of describing position on a globe and underlies every topographic map, which is why it opens the mapping laboratory.

## The Two Coordinates

| Coordinate | Measures | Reference line | Range | Lines called |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Latitude** | Angular distance north or south | The **Equator** (0°) | 0° to 90° N or S | **Parallels** |
| **Longitude** | Angular distance east or west | The **Prime Meridian** (0°) | 0° to 180° E or W | **Meridians** |

**Parallels** of latitude run east–west and never intersect — they are true parallels, and they shrink toward the poles. **Meridians** of longitude run north–south from pole to pole; they are equally spaced at the Equator and converge to a point at each pole.

Always state the hemisphere. `30° N, 150° W` and `30° S, 150° E` are opposite corners of the planet.

## Degrees, Minutes, Seconds

For finer precision each degree is subdivided like a clock:

- 1 degree (°) = **60 minutes** (′)
- 1 minute (′) = **60 seconds** (″)

So a position may read `29° 44′ 12″ N, 95° 47′ 30″ W`. Decimal degrees (29.7367° N) are an equivalent alternative; to convert, multiply the decimal remainder by 60 for minutes, then that remainder by 60 again for seconds.

## Quadrangles

A **quadrangle** is a four-sided area bounded by two parallels of latitude and two meridians of longitude. Because meridians converge poleward, a quadrangle is not a true rectangle — it is slightly narrower at its poleward edge.

Quadrangles are the standard unit for published topographic map sheets. The USGS **7.5-minute quadrangle** covers 7.5 minutes of latitude by 7.5 minutes of longitude and is the workhorse sheet for detailed geologic mapping in the United States.

To describe a quadrangle, give its **bounding latitudes and longitudes** — for example, an area bounded by the Equator and 30° S and by 60° E and 90° E.

## Three Ways to State a Location

The mapping lab opens by asking for three different answers to "where are you?" — the point being that location can be described at different levels of precision and purpose:

1. **Political/postal** — address, city, state, country
2. **Relative** — position described against a landmark ("30 miles west of downtown Houston")
3. **Absolute/coordinate** — latitude and longitude

Only the third is unambiguous worldwide and machine-readable, which is why it underlies all mapping.

## Elevation as the Third Dimension

Latitude and longitude fix a position on the surface; **elevation** above sea level supplies the third dimension. On a topographic map elevation is carried by contour lines — see [[Topographic Maps]].
