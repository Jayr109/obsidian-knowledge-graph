As glaciers move and melt, they carve distinctive **erosional landforms** and lay down equally distinctive **depositional landforms**. Valley glaciers sharpen topography into angular peaks and troughs, while ice sheets tend to subdue the land and blanket it with sediment. This page catalogs both sets of features; the glaciers themselves are covered on [[Glaciers]].

## How Glaciers Erode

- **Plucking** — meltwater seeps into cracks and joints of bedrock beneath the glacier, freezes, and lifts out blocks.
- **Abrasion** — rock embedded in the ice grinds the bed like sandpaper, producing **rock flour** (pulverized rock) and **glacial striations** (grooves recording ice-flow direction).

Erosion depends on the ice's velocity and thickness, the rock fragments it carries, and the erodibility of the bed.

## Erosional Landforms

- **U-shaped trough** — a valley widened and deepened into a U by a valley glacier (vs. the V-shape cut by streams).
- **Hanging valleys** — tributary valleys left high above the main trough, often with waterfalls.
- **Truncated spurs** — ridge ends sheared off as the glacier straightens the valley.
- **Cirque** — a bowl-shaped depression at the head of a glacial valley; after melting it may hold a small lake called a **tarn**. A chain of such lakes down a valley is **paternoster lakes**.
- **Arête** — a sharp, knife-edged ridge between two cirques; a **col** is a gap where opposing cirques meet.
- **Horn** — a pyramid-shaped peak carved by cirques on several sides (e.g., the Matterhorn).
- **Roches moutonnées** — an asymmetric bedrock knob: **abrasion** smooths the gentle up-glacier (stoss) side and **plucking** steepens the down-glacier (lee) side.
- **Fjords** — deep, steep-sided sea inlets that are drowned glacial troughs flooded by rising sea level (depths can exceed 1,000 m).

## Glacial Deposits: Drift, Till, and Stratified Drift

**Glacial drift** is the general term for all sediment of glacial origin. It comes in two kinds:
- **Till** — unsorted, unlayered material deposited **directly by the ice**. Large boulders carried far from their source and left in or on the till are **glacial erratics**.
- **Stratified drift** — sorted, layered sediment laid down by **glacial meltwater**.

(Till makes a poor, clay-rich aquitard, while sorted outwash makes a good aquifer — see [[Groundwater]].)

## Landforms Made of Till

- **Lateral moraine** — debris ridge along the side of a valley glacier.
- **Medial moraine** — forms when two glaciers merge and their inner lateral moraines join down the center.
- **End (terminal) moraine** — a ridge of till at the glacier's terminus, marking its farthest advance.
- **Ground moraine** — a gently rolling till layer left as the ice front recedes.
- **Drumlins** — streamlined, asymmetric till hills (formed under ice sheets); the **steep side faces the direction the ice came from**, the gentle slope points the way it moved. They cluster in **drumlin fields**.

## Landforms Made of Stratified Drift

- **Outwash plains** (beyond ice sheets) and **valley trains** (in mountain valleys) — broad, ramp-like surfaces of meltwater-deposited, braided-stream sediment.
- **Kettles** — basins left where buried blocks of ice melted, pockmarking outwash.
- **Kames** — steep-sided mounds of ice-contact stratified drift.
- **Eskers** — long, sinuous ridges of sand and gravel deposited by meltwater streams flowing within or beneath the ice.
