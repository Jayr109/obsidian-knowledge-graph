**Coastal processes** are the work of waves, currents, and tides that continuously reshape shorelines through a shifting "battle" between **erosion** and **deposition**. Because ~75% of the U.S. population lives in coastal areas, these processes are both geologically important and a major hazard concern. This page covers wave dynamics, sediment transport, and the erosional and depositional landforms of the shore; the hazards and engineering responses are on [[Coastal Hazards and Protection]].

## Coastal Definitions

- **Coast** — the zone from the water to a major change in landforms inland.
- **Shore** — the zone between high and low water.
- **Beach** — sediment in transit along the shore (a "river of sand").
- **Longshore current** — a current flowing parallel to the coast that transports sediment along the shore.

## Waves

Waves form from the **frictional drag of wind** on the water surface. Their size depends on three things:
- **Wind velocity**, **wind duration**, and **fetch** (the distance the wind blows across open water).

Key terms: the **crest** and **trough** are the high and low points; **wave height** is the vertical distance between them; **wavelength** is the horizontal distance between crests; **wave period** is the time for one wavelength to pass; and **wave velocity = wavelength ÷ period**. Wave motion dies out downward at the **wave base** (≈ ½ the wavelength). Importantly, when wave **height doubles, energy increases fourfold**.

As a wave enters shallow water it "feels bottom," slows, shortens, and steepens until it collapses as a **breaker**; the turbulent water of broken waves is the **surf**.

## Wave Refraction and Sediment Transport

- **Wave refraction** is the bending of waves as they approach shore. It **concentrates energy on headlands** and **weakens it in bays**, so over time refraction **straightens irregular shorelines**.
- Because waves usually approach at an angle, sediment moves along the beach face in a zig-zag called **beach drift**, while the **longshore current** carries suspended sand along the coast.
- **Rip currents** are narrow, concentrated flows of backwash that rush seaward through gaps in offshore sandbars. They cause ~100 U.S. deaths per year — escape by swimming **parallel** to the beach until free of the current.

## Erosional Landforms

- **Wave-cut cliff** — cut by surf undercutting the base of the coast.
- **Wave-cut platform** — the flat bench left behind as the cliff recedes; a **marine terrace** is a wave-cut platform later uplifted by tectonics.
- **Sea cave → sea arch → sea stack** — soft/fractured rock in a headland erodes into caves; when two caves meet, a **sea arch** forms; when the arch collapses, an isolated **sea stack** remains.

## Depositional Landforms

- **Spit** — an elongated sand ridge extending from land into a bay mouth.
- **Baymouth bar** — a spit that grows all the way across a bay, sealing it off.
- **Tombolo** — a sand ridge connecting an island to the mainland.
- **Barrier islands** — low offshore sand ridges paralleling the coast (mainly the Atlantic and Gulf coastal plains); narrow, temporary, and very vulnerable to storms.

## Coastal Classification

- **Emergent coasts** — produced by uplift or a fall in sea level; marked by wave-cut cliffs and **marine terraces** (e.g., the California coast).
- **Submergent coasts** — produced by subsidence or a rise in sea level; marked by drowned river mouths (**estuaries**) and highly irregular shorelines (e.g., the Atlantic Coast).
