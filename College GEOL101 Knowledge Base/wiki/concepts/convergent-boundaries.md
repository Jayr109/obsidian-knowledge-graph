A **convergent boundary** is where two lithospheric plates move toward each other. Depending on the type of crust involved, the result is subduction with volcanic arcs and deep-ocean trenches, or the collision of continents into mountain belts. It is one of the three boundary types in [[Plate Tectonics]], with [[Divergent Boundaries]] and [[Transform Boundaries]].

## Why Only Oceanic Crust Subducts

Continental lithosphere is **thicker and less dense**; oceanic lithosphere is **thinner and denser**. As a rule, only the denser **oceanic** lithosphere can subduct (descend) into the mantle.

## Three Subtypes

| Subtype | What happens | Result | Examples |
|---------|-------------|--------|----------|
| **Oceanic–Continental** | Dense oceanic slab subducts beneath continent; released water triggers partial melting | **Continental volcanic arc** | Andes, Cascade Range |
| **Oceanic–Oceanic** | One oceanic slab descends beneath another | **Volcanic island arc** + deep trench | Aleutian Islands, Mariana Islands |
| **Continental–Continental** | Neither plate subducts; crust crumples | **Mountain belt** of deformed rock | Himalayas, Alps, Appalachians |

## Key Features

- **Deep-ocean trenches** mark where slabs descend — e.g., the Peru-Chile Trench, Mariana Trench, Tonga Trench.
- Partial melting is triggered where the subducting slab reaches **~100 km depth**, feeding the volcanoes of the overlying arc (see [[Magma]] and [[Volcanoes]]).
- Convergent margins host the deepest earthquakes (down to ~700 km) and the highest-magnitude earthquakes on Earth (see [[Earthquakes]]).
