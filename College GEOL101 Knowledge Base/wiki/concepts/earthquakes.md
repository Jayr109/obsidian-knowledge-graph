An **earthquake** is the vibration of Earth produced by the rapid release of energy stored in rocks, most commonly caused by slip along a fault. Earthquakes are one of Earth's most destructive natural hazards and are strongly concentrated at plate boundaries, though they can occur anywhere stress is stored in the lithosphere.

## Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Fault** | Fracture in Earth along which movement has occurred |
| **Focus (hypocenter)** | Point inside Earth where the earthquake originates |
| **Epicenter** | Point on Earth's surface directly above the focus |
| **Fault scarp** | Cliff formed by displacement along a fault |
| **Fault trace** | Line where fault plane meets the surface; rocks are shattered, often forming lakes |
| **Foreshocks** | Smaller quakes preceding the main event |
| **Aftershocks** | Smaller quakes following the main event |

## Elastic Rebound Theory

Developed following the **1906 San Francisco earthquake** (estimated 7.9 Mw):

1. Rocks near plate boundaries experience stress and deform **elastically**, storing energy.
2. Strain accumulates over years to centuries.
3. When rock strength is exceeded, rupture occurs along a fault → stored energy releases as seismic waves.
4. Unfractured rock snaps back toward its original shape (elastic rebound).

## Causes Beyond Faulting
- Magma chamber inflation (volcanoes)
- Volcanic eruptions
- Meteorite impacts
- **Human activity**: injection wells (fracking), water-reservoir impoundment → **induced seismicity** (intraplate example).

## Earthquake Distribution and Plate Tectonics

| Boundary Type | Stress | Fault Type | Depth | Magnitude Potential |
|--------------|--------|-----------|-------|---------------------|
| Convergent (subduction) | Compression | Reverse / thrust | 0–700 km | Highest (>9.0 Mw possible) |
| Divergent | Tension | Normal | <10 km | Low–moderate |
| Transform | Shear | Strike-slip | ~20 km | Moderate–large |

**Intraplate earthquakes** occur away from boundaries:
- Reactivated ancient faults (New Madrid, Missouri — 1811–1812, up to 7.7 Mw)
- Hot spots (magma movement)
- Human-induced activity

## Magnitude Scales

### Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale
Qualitative; based on observed damage and felt reports. Ranges I–XII. Useful for historical events.

### Richter Magnitude (1935)
Logarithmic; measures the **largest wave amplitude** on a seismogram corrected for distance.
- 1 unit = 10× amplitude, ~32× energy.
- Works best for moderate, nearby earthquakes.

### Moment Magnitude (Mw) — Now Standard
Quantifies **total energy released**: average fault slip × fault area × rock rigidity.
- More accurate for large and distant events.
- Backward compatible with Richter for moderate earthquakes.

## Notable Historical Earthquakes

| Event | Mw | Deaths | Significance |
|-------|----|--------|-------------|
| T'ang Shan, China, 1976 | ~7.8 | 240,000+ | Deadliest 20th-century quake |
| Lisbon, Portugal, 1755 | ~8.5–9.0 | 60,000+ | Led to early seismology |
| New Madrid, MO, 1811–1812 | 7.7 | Few (sparse pop.) | Largest intraplate US events |
| San Francisco, 1906 | ~7.9 | 3,000+ | Fire caused most deaths; led to elastic rebound theory |
| Good Friday, Alaska, 1964 | 9.2 | 139 | Largest US earthquake recorded |
| Banda Aceh, Sumatra, 2004 | 9.1 | 227,898 | 3rd-largest recorded; devastating tsunami |
| Tohoku, Japan, 2011 | 9.0 | 20,896 | Fukushima nuclear crisis |
| Nepal (Gorkha), 2015 | 7.8 | 9,000+ | Devastated cultural heritage |
| Haiti, 2010 | 7.0 | 316,000+ | Inequality amplified death toll |

## Future Threats

### San Andreas Fault (California)
- 62% probability of >6.7 Mw event in San Francisco Bay Area by 2032.
- **Seismic gap** north of Loma Prieta suggests overdue rupture.

### Cascadia Subduction Zone (Pacific Northwest)
- 800-mile subduction zone; last full rupture January 26, 1700 (9.0 Mw).
- 10% chance of >9.0 Mw in next 50 years.
- Would threaten ~11 million people in Vancouver, Victoria, Seattle, Portland, Sacramento.
- 80–90% chance of simultaneously triggering a major San Andreas event.
