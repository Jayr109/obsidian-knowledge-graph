**Continental drift** is the hypothesis — proposed by **Alfred Wegener** in 1915 — that Earth's continents were once joined in a single supercontinent and have since moved to their present positions. Initially rejected, it was the direct intellectual ancestor of modern [[Plate Tectonics]], vindicated decades later by [[Seafloor Spreading]] and [[Paleomagnetism]].

## Wegener's Hypothesis

- In *The Origin of Continents and Oceans* (1915), Alfred Wegener proposed the supercontinent **Pangaea** ("all lands"), which began fragmenting **~200 Ma** (the Mesozoic).
- Until the late 1960s, most geologists believed the continents were fixed in place.

## Lines of Evidence

1. **Continental fit** — the coastlines of continents (e.g., South America and Africa) fit together like a jigsaw puzzle.
2. **Matching rock records** — rock types and ages align across now-separated continents.
3. **Fossil record** — identical fossil organisms occur on continents now separated by oceans (they could not have swum across).
4. **Matching mountain ranges** — belts of similar age and structure line up across the North Atlantic.
5. **Glacial and coal-swamp distributions** — ancient glacial deposits and tropical coal swamps make sense only if the continents were once positioned differently.

## Why It Was Rejected

Wegener could not identify a credible **mechanism** for moving continents. His proposals (gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun, continents plowing through ocean crust like icebreakers) were physically implausible, so the scientific community dismissed the idea until a workable mechanism — mantle-driven [[Seafloor Spreading]] — was discovered.
