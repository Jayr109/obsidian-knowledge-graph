**Seismic waves** are the energy waves released by an earthquake that travel outward in all directions from the focus (hypocenter). They are recorded by seismographs and used to locate earthquake epicenters, measure magnitude, and image Earth's interior.

## Types of Seismic Waves

### Body Waves (travel through Earth's interior)

**Primary Waves (P-waves / Compressional Waves)**
- **Fastest** seismic waves — arrive at seismograph first.
- Travel through **solids, liquids, and gases**.
- Particle motion is **parallel** to wave propagation direction (alternating compression and expansion — like a Slinky pushed end-to-end).
- Can pass through Earth's liquid outer core → used to image Earth's interior.

**Secondary Waves (S-waves / Shear Waves)**
- **Slower** than P-waves — arrive second.
- Travel only through **solid materials** (cannot travel through liquids or gases).
- Particle motion is **perpendicular** to wave propagation (up-down or side-to-side motion).
- Fact that S-waves stop at the core proved the outer core is liquid.

### Surface Waves

- **Slowest** of all seismic waves.
- Travel along Earth's surface rather than through the interior.
- Cause both **horizontal and vertical rolling motion**.
- Responsible for **most structural damage** during earthquakes — buildings sway and roll.
- Larger amplitude than body waves near the surface.

## Comparison Table

| Wave Type | Speed | Travel Medium | Motion | Damage Potential |
|-----------|-------|--------------|--------|-----------------|
| P-wave | Fastest | Solid, liquid, gas | Parallel (push-pull) | Low |
| S-wave | Medium | Solid only | Perpendicular (shear) | Moderate |
| Surface wave | Slowest | Surface of Earth | Rolling/horizontal | **Highest** |

## Seismographs and Seismograms

- **Seismograph**: detects and records ground motion. A heavy mass suspended by a spring remains stationary while the frame (bolted to the ground) moves; the relative motion traces a record.
- **Seismogram**: the paper or electronic record produced; shows arrival of P-waves, then S-waves, then surface waves.
- First earthquake-detecting device: China, **132 BCE**.

## Locating an Epicenter (Triangulation)

The **P–S time interval** on a seismogram is the key measurement:
1. Measure the time gap between first P-wave arrival and first S-wave arrival.
2. Plot this interval on a **travel-time graph** → reads off the distance to the epicenter.
3. Draw a circle of that radius around the seismograph station.
4. Repeat for **at least 3 stations** → circles intersect at the epicenter.

A 7-minute P–S interval corresponds to approximately **5,300 km** from the station.

## Use in Earth's Interior Studies

- P-waves bend and slow as they enter the dense mantle → reveals mantle structure.
- P-waves stop at the liquid outer core, creating a **shadow zone**.
- S-waves do not pass through the outer core → confirms it is liquid.
- P-waves speed up in the solid inner core → reveals its existence.
